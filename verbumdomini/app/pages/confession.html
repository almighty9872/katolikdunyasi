Confession

Caravaggio · The Denial of Saint Peter

In confession you tell your sins to a priest, and God forgives them. It takes a few minutes. Jesus gave this power to the apostles on the evening of the Resurrection: “If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven” (John 20:22-23). The priest is the instrument. The one who forgives you is God.

Find the confession times first. Most parishes hear confessions once or twice a week, often on Saturday afternoon. The times are on the parish website or in the bulletin. If none of them suit you, call the parish office and ask the priest for an appointment. In Advent and Lent many parishes add extra times.

About five minutes

Step by step

Confession has the same shape every time. Once you know it, there is nothing left to be afraid of. If you forget a step, the priest will guide you.

  1. 1

    Before you go: examine your conscience

    Sit quietly, in the church or at home. Ask the Holy Spirit to help you see your life honestly. Then think back over what you have done and failed to do since your last confession.

    What you do: go through the examination of conscience below. Write your sins down on paper or on your phone if that helps.

  2. 2

    Go in

    Wait your turn near the confessional. When it is free, go in. You can kneel behind the screen, where the priest cannot see you, or sit facing him. Both are fine.

    What you do: choose screen or face to face. The priest may greet you or read a short passage from Scripture.

  3. 3

    Begin

    Make the Sign of the Cross, then say how long it has been since your last confession. If you do not remember, a guess is fine. If it is your first time, say so.

    “Bless me, Father, for I have sinned. It has been three months since my last confession.”

  4. 4

    Say your sins

    Say them simply and plainly. For serious sins, say what they were and, if you can, roughly how many times. You do not need to tell the whole story or defend yourself. When you have finished, say so.

    “For these and all the sins of my past life, I am sorry.”

  5. 5

    Listen to the priest

    He may give you a word of advice or ask a question. Then he gives you a penance, usually a prayer or a good deed. It is not a punishment. It is your first step back toward God.

    What you do: listen, and remember the penance. If you did not hear it, ask him to repeat it.

  6. 6

    Say you are sorry: the Act of Contrition

    The priest asks you to pray an act of contrition. Use your own words, a short prayer, or the traditional one. You may read it from a card or your phone.

    “O my God, I am heartily sorry for having offended thee, and I detest all my sins because of thy just punishments, but most of all because they offend thee, my God, who art all good and deserving of all my love. I firmly resolve, with the help of thy grace, to sin no more, and to avoid the near occasions of sin. Amen.”

    Short version: “Lord Jesus, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner.” Vatican News has more acts of contrition ↗.

  7. 7

    Absolution: you are forgiven

    The priest extends his hand over you and says the words of absolution. With these words every sin you confessed is forgiven, completely (CCC 1441-1442).

    “God, the Father of mercies, through the death and resurrection of his Son has reconciled the world to himself and sent the Holy Spirit among us for the forgiveness of sins; through the ministry of the Church may God give you pardon and peace, and I absolve you from your sins in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.”

    What you say: “Amen.” Make the Sign of the Cross.

  8. 8

    Go in peace

    The priest sends you out, often with “Give thanks to the Lord, for he is good,” to which you answer “His mercy endures for ever.” Or he simply says “Go in peace.”

    What you do: do your penance as soon as you can, ideally before you leave the church. Then thank God.

Preparation

Examination of conscience

The questions follow the Ten Commandments. Open one at a time and read slowly. When a question stings, note it down: that is what you bring to confession.

Serious sins first. You must confess every serious (mortal) sin you remember. Smaller (venial) sins are good to confess too, but they are not required (CCC 1456-1458).

Temptation is not sin. An unwanted thought or temptation is not a sin; consenting to it is. For a sin to be mortal it must be a serious matter, done with full knowledge and deliberate consent. These questions name what can be wrong; they do not decide how guilty you are (CCC 1857-1859).

The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1-17; Deuteronomy 5:6-21). Tap one to see its questions.

1. I am the LORD your God: you shall not have other gods before me

God comes first. You don’t have to bow to an idol to break this one: putting money, success, fame or a person in God’s place does it too. So do superstitions like fortune-telling, horoscopes, magic and lucky charms (CCC 2110-2128).

  • Have I neglected prayer? Did I remember God only in hard times?
  • Have I put my trust in fortune-tellers, horoscopes, magic or lucky charms?
  • Have money, career, being liked or a relationship taken God’s place in my life?
  • Have I been ashamed of my faith or hidden it?
  • Have I knowingly and stubbornly rejected a truth the Church teaches?
  • Have I despaired of God’s mercy, or sinned deliberately thinking “he’ll forgive me anyway”?
  • Have I received Communion knowing I had a mortal sin I had not confessed?
  • Have I treated the Eucharist, the church or blessed objects without reverence?
  • Have I used a Ouija board or a medium, or tried to contact the dead?
  • Have I believed that all religions are equally true?
  • Have I neglected to learn my faith?

2. You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain

It asks for reverence for God’s name and for what is holy. Blasphemy, swearing by God’s name needlessly or falsely, breaking a vow made to God and mocking religion go against it (CCC 2142-2159).

  • Have I used the name of God, Jesus or Mary in anger, or blasphemed?
  • Have I sworn by God’s name needlessly or falsely?
  • Have I broken a promise or vow I made to God?
  • Have I mocked religion, holy things or believers, or laughed along when others did?

3. Remember to keep holy the Lord's Day

It asks us to give Sunday to God and to rest. For Catholics this means taking part in Mass on Sundays and holy days of obligation. Avoiding needless work and making time for family, rest and good works are part of it too (CCC 2180-2188).

  • Have I missed Mass on a Sunday or holy day of obligation without a valid reason?
  • Have I come late to Mass on purpose, or spent it on my phone?
  • Did I spend Sunday on work, shopping and screens? Did I make time for my family and for rest?
  • Have I kept others (employees, family) from resting on Sunday?
  • Have I talked during Mass or distracted others?

4. Honor your father and your mother

It starts with our parents and extends to everyone with responsibility over us, at home, at work and in society. It runs both ways: parents owe their children, employers owe their workers, and grown children owe their aging parents (CCC 2197-2233).

  • Have I been rude, disrespectful or ungrateful to my parents? Have I neglected to call or visit them?
  • Have I given elderly or sick family members the care they need?
  • If I am a parent, have I made time for my children and given them the faith and a good example?
  • Have I treated my spouse and family with patience and love?
  • Have I respected my teachers, my superiors and the law, as long as they don’t ask me to act against my conscience?
  • Have I been too harsh or unfair with my children? Have I watched what they see and who they spend time with?
  • Have I punished my spouse with silence, sulking or jealousy?
  • If I have authority at work, have I treated anyone unjustly?
  • If I am a student, have I neglected my studies?

5. You shall not kill

It covers not only murder but everything that harms human life and health: anger, hatred and the wish for revenge, humiliating or bullying someone, abortion, drugs, drunkenness, reckless driving and neglecting one’s own health. Jesus says that whoever is angry with his brother will also be judged (Matthew 5:21-22; CCC 2258-2317).

  • Have I shouted at, insulted or humiliated someone in anger? Have I held on to hatred or a wish for revenge?
  • Have I deliberately excluded or bullied someone (online too)?
  • Have I harmed my own health through excessive alcohol, smoking, drugs or carelessness?
  • Have I endangered myself and others by speeding or driving carelessly?
  • Have I had an abortion, or pushed someone toward having one?
  • Have I led someone into sin by my bad example?
  • Have I refused to forgive, or turned away someone who wanted to make peace?
  • Have I hit or hurt anyone?
  • Have I taken part in or encouraged euthanasia or assisted suicide?

Good to know. Suicide is gravely wrong, but fear, deep distress and illness can lessen a person’s responsibility. The Church prays for those who have taken their own lives and does not despair of their salvation (CCC 2282-2283).

6. You shall not commit adultery

It teaches that sexuality belongs to the lifelong, total gift of love between husband and wife in marriage. Sex outside marriage, adultery, pornography, masturbation, artificial contraception in marriage and treating one’s spouse as an object go against it. Married or single, everyone is called to live chastely according to their state of life (CCC 2337-2359, 2380-2391).

  • Have I watched pornography or looked for explicit content?
  • Have I had sexual relations outside marriage?
  • If I am married, have I been unfaithful to my spouse? Have I treated my spouse as an object?
  • Have I masturbated?
  • Have I led others into sin by the way I dress, my jokes or my messages?

Good to know. Same-sex attraction is not itself a sin; these questions are about acts (CCC 2358). A civil divorce is not always a sin: it can be allowed when it is the only way to protect yourself, your children or your rights (CCC 2383).

7. You shall not steal

Respect what belongs to others, and their work. Stealing isn’t only taking things: slacking off on the clock, cheating customers, dodging taxes, not paying debts, pirating software or films and keeping something you found all count. Helping the poor and caring for creation are part of it too (CCC 2401-2449).

  • Have I taken something that belongs to someone else, failed to return what I borrowed or to pay a debt?
  • Have I taken pay without working, wasted work time, or used my employer’s property for myself?
  • Have I cheated in buying, selling or my taxes? Have I paid my employees fully and on time?
  • Have I used pirated software, films or music?
  • Have I neglected my family’s needs through gambling or needless spending?
  • Have I helped the poor and those in need as much as I can?
  • Have I cheated at school or work, or passed off someone else’s work as mine?
  • Have I given or taken a bribe?
  • Have I damaged something and not made it right?

8. You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor

It asks for respect for the truth. Lying, slander and detraction (needlessly telling others about someone’s real but hidden faults) go against it. Judging someone without hearing them out, revealing secrets and spreading unverified claims on social media belong here too (CCC 2475-2492).

  • Have I lied, or twisted or exaggerated the truth?
  • Have I talked behind someone’s back, needlessly telling others their faults?
  • Have I slandered someone or accused them unjustly?
  • Have I judged someone without hearing them out, only from what I had heard?
  • Have I revealed a secret entrusted to me?
  • Have I shared things on social media without being sure they were true?
  • Have I boasted, or flattered someone to get something?
  • Have I tried to repair someone’s good name after I damaged it?

9 and 10. You shall not covet your neighbor's spouse or goods*

The last two commandments look not at actions but at the heart. The ninth forbids desiring another person’s spouse; the tenth forbids envy of other people’s goods and greed. Jesus says that whoever looks at a woman with lust has already committed adultery in his heart (Matthew 5:28; CCC 2514-2557).

  • Have I deliberately dwelt on lustful thoughts and fantasies?
  • Have I looked with desire at someone else’s spouse or partner?
  • Have I envied other people’s homes, cars, jobs or lives? Have I grown discontented looking at others’ lives on social media?
  • Have I been too attached to money and things? Have I thanked God for what I have?
  • Have I been glad at someone else’s misfortune?

* Why are 9 and 10 together? The Bible doesn’t number the commandments, so different traditions count them differently. Catholics follow St. Augustine: the ban on other gods and on idols is one commandment, the first, and the ban on coveting is split in two, your neighbor’s spouse (ninth) and your neighbor’s goods (tenth) (Deuteronomy 5:21). Most Orthodox and Protestant churches count idols separately and join the two on coveting. We put the last two together here because both are about what goes on in the heart before anything is done (CCC 2514-2557).

People who came back

In their words

Questions and fears

Good to know

Will the priest tell anyone what I say?

Never. The seal of confession is absolute: a priest may never reveal anything he hears there, to anyone, for any reason, not even to save his own life (CCC 1467). Some priests have died rather than break it.*

Will the priest judge me or get angry?

No. The priest acts in the person of Christ, not in his own name; the forgiveness you hear is Christ’s. He has heard it all before, and most priests are glad you found the courage to come.

It's been years, and I don't know how to begin

Tell the priest: “Father, it’s been a long time. Can you help me?” He has heard that many times. He will be glad you came, and he will walk you through it. You do not have to remember every sin of those years exactly: confess the serious ones you remember, and roughly how often.

What if I forget what to say, or forget a sin?

The priest will help, and ask you questions if needed. You can read your list from paper or your phone. If you honestly forget a sin, it is forgiven with the others. If it was serious, mention it next time.

There is one sin I'm too ashamed to say

Say it anyway, as plainly as you can, and get it over with. The priest will not be shocked. If you knowingly hold back a serious sin, the confession does not help you, because you have not really handed it over (CCC 1456). Many people find that the sin they dreaded saying is the one they feel freest from afterwards.

What's the difference between mortal and venial sin?

A sin is mortal when three things come together: the matter is grave, you know it, and you choose it freely (CCC 1857). If any of the three is missing, the sin is venial. If you are not sure, ask the priest.

How often should I go?

The Church asks you to confess serious sins at least once a year (CCC 1457). If you are aware of a mortal sin, go before you receive Communion again. Many Catholics go once a month, and find it keeps them honest and at peace.

I keep confessing the same sins. Is that a problem?

No, that is normal. Most people struggle with the same few things for years. Holiness takes time. The point is not to arrive perfect; it is to come back to God every time.

Screen or face to face?

Whichever you prefer. Both are valid and both are common. Church law asks every church to have a confessional with a screen for anyone who wants one (canon 964).

How long does it take?

Usually a few minutes. If you have a lot to say, or it has been many years, it is kind to make an appointment rather than use a busy Saturday slot.

Can I go if I'm not baptized, or not Catholic?

Confession is for baptized Catholics. If you have never been baptized, baptism itself washes away every sin. If you were baptized in another church and want to become Catholic, you make your first confession before you are received. See How to Become Catholic. You can always talk to a priest outside confession, and ask for his blessing.

* Martyrs of the seal

A few of the priests killed for refusing to reveal what they heard in confession:

  • St. John Nepomucene (d. 1393) Often called the first martyr of the seal. King Wenceslaus IV of Bohemia had him tortured and thrown into the Vltava in Prague for refusing to reveal the queen’s confessions.
  • St. Mateo Correa Magallanes (d. 1927) A Mexican priest executed during the Cristero War. General Eulogio Ortiz ordered him to hear the confessions of imprisoned Cristero rebels and then demanded to know what they had said. Magallanes refused and was shot.
  • Blessed Jan Sarkander (d. 1620) A priest in Moravia during the Thirty Years' War. He was tortured to death on the rack over several days for refusing to reveal the confessional secrets of a Catholic nobleman to opposing authorities.
  • Blessed Felipe Císcar Puig (d. 1936) A Spanish priest executed during the Spanish Civil War. He was shot by Republican forces after refusing to reveal the confession of a Franciscan priest he had just heard in prison.
