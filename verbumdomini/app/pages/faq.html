Common Misunderstandings

Do Catholics worship Mary, the saints or statues?

No. Worship belongs to God alone. Mary and the saints are honored, not worshipped. Statues and images point to the person they show; they have no power of their own.

Three kinds of honor. Worship (Latin latria) belongs to God alone; worshipping a creature is idolatry. Mary and the saints receive honor and love (dulia). Mary receives a higher degree of honor, called hyperdulia, but it is still not worship.

Asking a saint to “pray for us” is like asking a friend to pray for you. The saints are alive in Christ, and death does not divide the Church. Christ is the one Mediator, and the saints’ prayers reach the Father through Him. When Catholics pray to Mary, they ask her to pray to God for them.

Statues and images work the same way (canon 1188). The honor goes to the person they show, just as kissing a photo of someone you love isn’t about the paper. The image itself has no power.

The Old Testament ban on images was aimed at idols. The same God told Moses to make cherubim for the Ark and a bronze serpent (Exodus 25:18–20; Numbers 21:8–9). In Christ the invisible God became visible, so His face can be painted. The Second Council of Nicaea taught this in 787.

CCC 971, 1159–1162, 2096–2097, 2129–2132 · CIC can. 1186–1188

Do Catholics believe in three Gods?

No. There is one God. The Trinity means the one God exists as three Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

One and three, but not in the same way. This is no contradiction, because God is not “one” and “three” in the same way. He is one in nature and three in Persons. The Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Holy Spirit, and yet each is fully God. Each has the whole divine nature, not a third of it.

Human reason could never have discovered this alone. It is a mystery God revealed about Himself, and we accept it by faith.

CCC 232–234, 253–256, 266

Hasn’t the Bible been changed over the centuries, or kept from ordinary people?

No, on both counts. Thousands of ancient manuscripts agree, so the text has not been changed. And the Church never hid the Bible: it was costly before printing, and today every Catholic is urged to read it.

Changed? No, and you can check it yourself. The New Testament survives in more than five thousand Greek manuscripts, some from the 2nd century. The Dead Sea Scrolls gave us Hebrew texts of the Old Testament a thousand years older than any we had before. These copies come from different places and communities, and they agree. The differences are in spelling and word order, and no teaching depends on them.

Kept from the people? No. Before printing, monks copied each Bible by hand over many months, so a Bible cost a fortune. Churches chained them to the lectern so they could not be stolen and anyone could come and read them. Latin was the common language of educated Europe; that is why St. Jerome’s Latin Bible is called the Vulgate, the “common” Bible. Bishops banned some translations that had errors, not the Bible itself. Today the Church urges every Catholic to read Scripture, because “ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ” (St. Jerome).

CCC 105–108, 120, 131–133

Why not “the Bible alone,” and why do Protestant Bibles have fewer books?

The Bible itself doesn’t teach “Bible alone.” Scripture, Tradition and the Church’s teaching office work together. Seven Old Testament books were in the Greek Bible the apostles used and have always been Scripture; Protestants dropped them in the 1500s.

Bible alone? The Bible never says that Scripture alone is the only rule of faith. Paul writes: “hold to the traditions that you were taught by us, either by word of mouth or by our letter” (2 Thessalonians 2:15). He calls the Church, not the Bible, “the pillar and bulwark of the truth” (1 Timothy 3:15). Jesus wrote no book; He founded a Church, and the apostles first preached by word of mouth. The Bible also has no list of which books belong in it. The Church recognized that list at the councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397).

The deuterocanonical books. The Old Testament has always included seven books (Tobit, Judith, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach and Baruch) and longer versions of Esther and Daniel that Protestant Bibles leave out. “Deuterocanonical” means their place in the canon was settled later, not that they are lesser. They were in the Septuagint, the Greek Bible the apostles quoted most, and the Church has always used them. In the 1500s Luther moved them to an appendix because the Jewish Bible of his day did not have them, and later Protestant Bibles dropped them. Even the 1611 King James Version still printed them.

CCC 80–83, 95, 120, 138

Why do Catholics call priests “Father,” pray the Rosary and use a crucifix?

Each one points to Christ. A priest is a spiritual father, as Paul was. The Rosary prays Bible words while thinking about Jesus’ life. The crucifix shows what our salvation cost.

Calling a priest Father. “Call no man father” (Matthew 23:9). Here Jesus warns against pride and against putting anyone in God’s place. He does not ban the word: He speaks of honoring “your father and mother” (Matthew 15:4), and Paul writes, “I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel” (1 Corinthians 4:15). A priest is called Father because He gives spiritual life through the sacraments.

The Rosary. Jesus warned against “empty phrases,” praying as if many words could force God to listen (Matthew 6:7). He did not forbid repeating a prayer: He prayed the same words three times in Gethsemane (Matthew 26:44), and in heaven the angels say “Holy, holy, holy” without end (Revelation 4:8). The Hail Mary comes from the Bible (Luke 1:28, 42), and while saying it we think about the life of Christ.

The crucifix. Many Protestants prefer an empty cross because Christ is risen. Catholics believe that too, but with Paul “we proclaim Christ crucified” (1 Corinthians 1:23). The crucifix shows the price of our salvation and how much God loves us.

CCC 971, 1548, 2678, 2708

Sacraments and Church Practices

Why do Catholics confess to a priest instead of going directly to God?

God forgives, through the priest. Christ gave the apostles power to forgive sins, and the priest acts in His person. Hearing “I absolve you” makes forgiveness certain, and the priest may never reveal what he hears.

Christ’s own plan. The question is not whether God forgives, but how He chose to give that forgiveness.

After the Resurrection, Christ breathed on the apostles and said: “Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained” (John 20:22–23). They could not decide to forgive or retain without hearing the sins. The priest acts in persona Christi, “in the person of Christ”: Christ forgives through him. The priest may never reveal what he hears (the seal of confession, canon 983).

We are bodies, not just souls, so we need to hear with our own ears that we are forgiven. “I suppose I’m forgiven” is not the same as hearing “I absolve you from your sins.”

Catholics also confess to God directly, and for venial sins that is enough. Mortal sin needs the sacrament (canon 960).

CCC 1441–1445, 1461–1467, 1484 · CIC can. 959–960, 983, 988

Are the bread and wine at Mass just a symbol of Jesus?

No. It is truly Christ. After the consecration the bread and wine still look the same, but they are now the Body and Blood of Christ.

Transubstantiation. The bread and wine still look, taste and test the same, but their substance, what they are, has changed. The Church calls this transubstantiation, a change of substance.

Christ is truly, really and substantially present: Body and Blood, Soul and Divinity (canon 899). Not part of Him, all of Him; not a symbol, Himself.

Jesus said, “This is my body” (Matthew 26:26). In John 6 He said, “Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you,” and many followers left Him. He didn’t call them back to say He meant it symbolically.

Christians have died for this belief. In 304, forty-nine Christians in Abitina, North Africa, were arrested for holding Sunday Mass against the emperor’s order. They told the judge, “Without Sunday we cannot live,” and were put to death. Saint Tarcisius, a young man in Rome, was killed rather than give up the Eucharist he was carrying to prisoners. People do not die for a symbol.

CCC 1374–1377, 1413 · CIC can. 897–899

Do I have to go to Mass every Sunday and keep the fasts?

Yes, both are Church precepts. Sunday Mass is required unless you have a serious reason. Fasting binds on Ash Wednesday and Good Friday, and Fridays are days of penance.

Sunday Mass. Catholics must attend Mass on Sundays and holy days of obligation (canon 1247); a Saturday evening Mass also counts (canon 1248). Missing it on purpose, without a serious reason, is a mortal sin. Serious reasons include illness, caring for small children or the sick, having no church nearby, or work you truly can’t avoid. If unsure, ask your pastor. This is how we keep the Third Commandment: “Remember the sabbath day, and keep it holy” (Exodus 20:8). If you love God, you can give Him one hour a week.

Fasting. In the Latin Church, Ash Wednesday and Good Friday are days of fasting and no meat (canon 1251). Fasting binds ages 18 to 59 and means one full meal plus two small ones. No meat binds from age 14 (canon 1252), and every Friday in Lent is meatless. Every Friday of the year is a day of penance, and in many countries the bishops let you replace the meat rule with another act of penance or charity (canon 1253).

Illness, pregnancy, hard physical work and similar reasons excuse you from fasting. With prayer and almsgiving, fasting keeps our appetites in order and makes room for God.

CCC 1438, 2042–2043, 2180–2183 · CIC can. 1246–1253

Who can receive Holy Communion?

Catholics in a state of grace. You must be a baptized Catholic, believe Christ is truly present, have no unconfessed mortal sin, and fast for one hour beforehand.

The details. “Full communion” means a baptized Catholic, not every baptized Christian. After a mortal sin, go to confession first (canon 916). In the one-hour fast, water and medicine don’t count (canon 919).

Asking non-Catholics not to receive is not a snub (canon 844). Communion expresses a unity that already exists; it doesn’t create one. Acting out a shared faith that isn’t shared yet wouldn’t be honest.

If you can’t receive, in many parishes, especially in North America, you can come forward with your arms crossed over your chest for a blessing. Where that isn’t the custom, stay in your pew and pray a spiritual communion.

CCC 1385, 1387, 1400, 1415, 1650 · CIC can. 844, 912, 915–916, 919

Authority and Church Teachings

Is the Pope sinless? What about claims that he is the Antichrist, or that Catholic customs are pagan?

No, and no. Popes sin and go to confession; infallibility covers only rare, final teachings. The Antichrist denies that Christ came in the flesh, which every Pope has taught. Customs that look pagan were given a Christian meaning.

Sinless? Infallibility and sinlessness are two very different things. Popes sin, popes go to confession, and some popes in history sinned badly.

Infallibility is very narrow. The Pope is infallible only when, as shepherd of the whole Church, he defines a teaching on faith or morals as final (ex cathedra, canon 749 §1). His politics, management, views on science and casual remarks are not covered. It has been used very rarely, and it does not let him invent new teaching; it only guarantees that he faithfully guards the faith handed down to him.

The Antichrist claim. The Bible defines the antichrist as one who denies that “Jesus Christ has come in the flesh” (2 John 7; 1 John 2:22). Every Pope has taught exactly that Jesus is God made man. The “great whore” of Babylon (Revelation 17) is best read as pagan Rome, “drunk with the blood of the saints,” which killed the first martyrs, Peter and Paul among them. Calling the Pope the Antichrist began in the bitter arguments of the 1500s.

The pagan claim. Christians did not copy pagan religion; they took ordinary human things, such as candles, incense, robes and feast days, and gave them to Christ. Paul did the same in Athens, using an altar “to an unknown god” to preach the true God (Acts 17:23). Easter comes from the Jewish Passover and the Gospel, not from pagan feasts. A custom that is older than Christianity no more makes you pagan than a wedding ring does, though rings were a Roman custom too.

CCC 675–677, 890–892, 1149 · CIC can. 331, 749 §1

How are the Orthodox different from Catholics?

Very close, but not yet united. The Orthodox have apostolic bishops and real sacraments. The split of 1054 is mainly about the Pope’s authority.

Apostolic roots. The Orthodox Churches also trace their bishops back to the apostles in an unbroken line (apostolic succession). The Church of Constantinople, for example, traces itself to St. Andrew, Peter’s brother. In Orthodox tradition he is called the “First-Called,” because he was the first of the disciples to follow Jesus (John 1:40–41).

Because of this, the Orthodox have valid sacraments, a real priesthood, the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist, and a deep devotion to Mary and the saints. We share a great deal. The split, which became final in 1054, is mainly about the authority of the Bishop of Rome, Peter’s successor, plus a few points of doctrine (such as the Filioque: whether the Holy Spirit also proceeds from the Son). The Church sees this split as a wound and prays for full unity.

CCC 816–819, 838

Does the Catholic Church think Protestants are of the devil?

No. The Church never taught that Protestants are of the devil. The harsh documents of the 1500s condemned ideas and the break, not people.

Why they broke away. Protestants broke away in the 1500s over four points: whether Scripture alone is the rule or Tradition and the Church’s teaching office also have authority; the number and nature of the sacraments; Christ’s real presence in the Eucharist; and how we are justified.

The harsh documents of the 1500s. The language of that age was fierce on both sides. Pope Leo X’s 1520 bull Exsurge Domine (“Arise, O Lord”) compared the new teaching to “the wild boar from the forest” destroying the Lord’s vineyard, and spoke of people “blinded in mind by the father of lies.” It condemned 41 of Luther’s propositions as “heretical, scandalous, false, offensive to pious ears or seductive of simple minds.” The Council of Trent (1545–1563) then issued more than a hundred canons with anathemas, formal condemnations of teachings such as justification by faith alone. Older catechisms and manuals classified Protestantism as heresy and schism.

Ideas, not people. These texts condemned teachings and the act of breaking away, not every Protestant. Canon law defines heresy as the obstinate denial of a truth of faith after baptism (canon 751), which requires knowing what one rejects. Someone raised in a Protestant family is in a very different place: “one cannot charge with the sin of the separation those who at present are born into these communities,” and the Church “accepts them with respect and affection as brothers” (CCC 818). The Second Vatican Council calls them “brothers in the Lord.” In 1999 Catholics and Lutherans even agreed that the 16th-century condemnations on justification do not apply to the Lutheran teaching as presented in their joint statement.

Every validly baptized Christian is in a real, if incomplete, communion with the Catholic Church (canon 205). Division is a scandal, and we are bound to pray for unity.

CCC 817–819, 838 · CIC can. 205, 751

What are Purgatory and indulgences?

Final cleansing before heaven. Everyone in Purgatory is saved; they are being made ready to see God. An indulgence is the Church’s help with that healing. Selling them was an abuse, long since banned.

Purgatory. It is not a “second chance” or a mild hell. Everyone in Purgatory is saved for good. It is the cleansing of people who die in God’s friendship but are not yet fully purified, so they can enter heaven. Being forgiven and being healed are two different things: a forgiven liar doesn’t lose the habit of lying overnight, and nothing unholy can see God. The Church has prayed for the dead from the beginning, which only makes sense if our prayers can still help them.

Indulgences. An indulgence is the Church’s help with that healing. God removes part or all of the remaining punishment for sins that are already forgiven (canon 992), usually in return for prayer, confession, Communion and acts of charity. It can be offered for the dead (canon 994). Luther was right that in his time some clergy sold indulgences; that was a real abuse. The Council of Trent (1562) abolished the preachers who collected money for indulgences, and in 1567 Pope Pius V banned tying an indulgence to any payment. Luther was wrong to throw out the teaching itself.

CCC 1030–1032, 1471–1479 · CIC can. 992–994

Does the Catholic Church reject science and the theory of evolution?

No. Faith and science don’t conflict. Catholics are free to study how life developed. Genesis teaches who made the world and why, not how.

One source of truth. All truth comes from the same God, so honest science can never truly contradict faith. Genesis isn’t a science book; it teaches that a good God made everything good and for a purpose.

The Church holds two things firmly: God created everything, and God creates each human soul directly. Science asks “how”; faith asks “why” and “by whom.”

History shows this. The universities grew out of the Church, and many founders of modern science were devout Catholics, some of them priests:

Nicolaus Copernicus, a church canon, showed that the earth goes around the sun. Gregor Mendel, an Augustinian friar, founded genetics. Fr. Georges Lemaître, a Belgian priest, first proposed the Big Bang theory. Louis Pasteur proved that germs cause disease and made the first rabies and anthrax vaccines. Blaise Pascal, André-Marie Ampère and Alessandro Volta laid foundations in mathematics, physics and electricity. Nicolas Steno, a father of geology, became a bishop. In our time, Jérôme Lejeune discovered the cause of Down syndrome.

The Church still runs the Vatican Observatory, one of the oldest in the world.

CCC 159, 283–284, 366

Can the Church’s core teachings of faith change over time?

No. Understanding grows; truth doesn’t. Revelation was complete in Christ. Teaching can deepen like a seed becoming a tree, but never contradict itself.

Growth, not change. Nothing new will be added to what God revealed in Christ. A seed growing into a tree looks different, but it is the same plant. A new teaching that made an old one false would be a break, and the Church has no power to do that.

Church rules can change, such as fasting rules, the language of the Mass or celibacy for Latin priests. Defined teaching on faith and morals cannot (canon 750).

CCC 66–67, 94 · CIC can. 747, 750

Faith and Reason: Common Objections

Why does God allow suffering and evil in the world?

There is no short answer. God does not want evil; most of it comes from misused freedom. And God did not stay distant: in Jesus He suffered with us and rose again.

The Catechism says no single sentence can answer it: the whole Christian faith together is the answer. Here are three parts of it.

God does not want evil. He does not cause it, and He did not make it. Evil is something good that is missing, the way blindness is missing sight.

Most evil comes from free choices. God made us free so that we can truly love, but people can misuse their freedom. If God stopped every bad choice, we would not really be free.

God came to suffer with us. The deepest Christian answer is not an idea but a person: Jesus. God did not watch our pain from far away. He became a man and suffered: He was hungry, betrayed, tortured, and He died on the cross. Then He rose from the dead, showing that suffering and death do not have the last word.

This does not make suffering good or explain all of it. But God can bring good even out of evil, and we will understand this fully only at the end of time.

CCC 309–314, 324, 385, 1500–1505

How can a loving God allow hell to exist?

God wants no one lost. Hell is the free choice to reject God to the end. God respects it, because forced love isn’t love.

No one is made for hell. God “desires everyone to be saved” (1 Timothy 2:4), and the Church prays for the salvation of every person.

God doesn’t send anyone to hell. People choose it by rejecting Him freely, to the very end. The root of that choice is pride, the first of the deadly sins. Pride puts self in God’s place and refuses His love, breaking the first commandment: “You shall have no other gods before me” (Exodus 20:3). Whoever rejects God to the end is saying, “I will not have you as my God,” and follows Satan, who fell through pride. God respects that choice, because forced love isn’t love. As the saying goes, the doors of hell are locked from the inside.

CCC 1033–1037, 1058, 1866, 2084, 2094

With so many religions, why Christianity? Can people who never heard of Christ be saved?

Because God came looking for us. In Christ God became man and rose from the dead. Those who never knew the Gospel through no fault of their own can still be saved by His grace.

Why Christianity. The Church respects what is true and holy in other religions. But Christianity makes a different claim. In other religions people search for God; in Christianity God comes looking for us and becomes a man. Jesus did not say He shows a way; He said, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life” (John 14:6). And the faith rests on a public event that can be examined, the Resurrection: “If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile” (1 Corinthians 15:17).

Salvation comes only through Christ, but people who don’t know the Gospel through no fault of their own, and who sincerely seek God and follow their conscience, can be saved. The grace that reaches them is Christ’s, even if they don’t know it.

Baptism of desire. Jesus commanded baptism (John 3:5), and it is necessary “by actual reception or at least by desire” (canon 849): “God has bound salvation to the sacrament of Baptism, but he himself is not bound by his sacraments.” A catechumen who dies before baptism, wanting it and sorry for sin, is saved by that desire. Someone who never heard the Gospel but does God’s will as they understand it would have wanted baptism had they known of it; this is an implicit desire. Those who die for Christ before baptism (baptism of blood) are saved as well.

The thief on the cross was never baptized. Dying, he admitted his guilt, defended Jesus as innocent and asked, “Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom.” Jesus answered: “Today you will be with me in Paradise” (Luke 23:39–43). It is never too late to turn to Christ, and God can save outside the sacraments. But this is no excuse to skip baptism: the thief had no chance to be baptized, and we do. Jesus gave the command to baptize all nations only after his Resurrection (Matthew 28:19).

None of this makes preaching pointless: the Church owes everyone the truth and the sure road of the sacraments. Children who die without baptism are entrusted to God’s mercy, and may have a Church funeral if their parents meant to baptize them (canon 1183 §2).

CCC 843–848, 1257–1261 · CIC can. 849, 1183

Don’t the Church’s sins, the Crusades and the Inquisition disprove its claim to be holy?

No. The Church is holy through Christ. Its members sin, and those sins betray its own standard. Crusade crimes and Inquisition executions were real, fewer than legend says, and the Church has asked forgiveness.

Holy, with sinful members. The Church is holy because of Christ, its Head, and the Holy Spirit, not because of its members. Its members, from laypeople to popes, can sin and have sinned. The Catechism calls the Church “at once holy and always in need of purification.” A teaching isn’t false because its followers fail to live it; a bad doctor doesn’t disprove medicine. These sins are scandals precisely because they betray the Church’s own standard, and Christ’s harshest words were for those who lead the little ones astray.

Wars, Crusades and the Inquisition. Religion is not the main cause of war: the bloodiest century, the 20th, was driven by nationalism and by atheist regimes like those of Stalin and Mao. The Crusades began in 1095, when the Christian East asked for help after Muslim armies had conquered Christian lands and attacked pilgrims. But crusaders also committed real crimes, such as the sack of Constantinople in 1204. The Inquisition was a system of Church courts for heresy cases. Historians now count its executions in the thousands over several centuries, far fewer than legend says, but each one was wrong. In 2000 John Paul II publicly asked forgiveness for these sins, including violence used “in the service of truth.”

CCC 827, 1428, 2104–2109

If God really exists, why doesn’t He show Himself plainly?

He shows Himself, but forces no one. God speaks through creation, conscience and Christ. Love must be free, so He gives light to those who seek without overwhelming those who don’t.

Not hiding. God shows Himself in creation, in conscience, in the history of Israel and most clearly in Christ. A sign in the sky would compel belief, but that would be submission, not love, and love must be free. So He gives enough light for anyone who seeks Him, but not so much that it blinds those who don’t want to see.

Faith isn’t a blind leap: it goes beyond reason, not against it. And Christians believe not only that seekers find God, but that God came looking for us first.

CCC 35–38, 143–150, 156–159

If Jesus is God, why did He cry out on the cross, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”

He was praying a psalm, not losing His faith. These are the first words of Psalm 22, a prayer that begins in anguish and ends in victory. The Father never abandoned His Son.

The first line of a psalm. Jesus is quoting Psalm 22:1 (Matthew 27:46, Mark 15:34). In Jewish teaching and oral tradition, a psalm was called to mind by its first line. Reciting that line pointed the listeners to the whole psalm and its context, much as naming the first words of a hymn brings the whole hymn to mind.

A psalm about the cross. Read the rest of Psalm 22 and it sounds like an account of Calvary: the mocking crowd shaking their heads, the pierced hands and feet, the soldiers casting lots for His garments (Matthew 27:39–43, John 19:23–24). Jesus is showing that the Scripture is being fulfilled in Him.

It does not end in despair. The psalm moves from anguish to praise, ending with God delivering the sufferer and all the ends of the earth turning to the Lord (Psalm 22:27). From the depth of His agony, Jesus points to the victory beyond it.

Did the Father turn away from Him? No. The Father and the Son are one God and can never be separated. Jesus was truly man and truly suffered, and He took our place: He carried the weight of our sin and our distance from God, and prayed in our name. He never stopped trusting the Father. His last words, in Luke, are a prayer of trust: “Father, into your hands I commend my spirit” (Luke 23:46).

CCC 603, 2605

Apologetics and Hard Questions

Did Christ really found the Catholic Church, or did Constantine?

Christ founded it, on Peter. Jesus called Peter the rock, and Peter led the Church and died in Rome. Constantine only made Christianity legal, nearly 300 years later.

Founded on Peter. Christ built His Church on Peter and the apostles: “You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it” (Matthew 16:18). He gave them His authority and sent them out (Matthew 28:18–20; John 20:21). The early Church had bishops who succeeded the apostles, and it looked to the Bishop of Rome as Peter’s successor (canons 330–331). A merely human institution would have collapsed long ago; the Church has lasted because Christ promised to stay with it.

Peter the rock. Jesus spoke Aramaic, in which “Peter” and “rock” are the same word, Kepha (John 1:42). The Greek Petros is simply the male form of petra, “rock,” used for a man’s name.

Peter in Rome. 1 Peter 5:13 sends greetings from “Babylon,” an early Christian code name for Rome. Christian writers of the first and second centuries say Peter taught and died there, and excavations found his tomb under St. Peter’s Basilica.

Constantine made Christianity legal in 313, almost 300 years after Pentecost, when Rome had already had more than thirty bishops in a row. The Council of Nicaea (325) did not invent doctrine; it defended what Christians already believed.

CCC 156, 552–553, 880–882 · CIC can. 330–331

Can Catholics get divorced?

A valid marriage can’t be dissolved. A Church court may find a marriage was never valid (an annulment). Divorced Catholics who have not remarried can still receive the sacraments.

Marriage is for life. A valid, consummated marriage between two baptized people cannot be dissolved by any human power (canon 1141). “What God has joined together, let no one separate” (Matthew 19:6). A civil divorce ends the marriage only in the eyes of the law.

A Church court can examine whether something essential was missing at the wedding (canon 1671). If so, it issues a declaration of nullity (an annulment): a finding that the marriage was never valid, not a Catholic divorce.

A divorced person who remarries without an annulment cannot receive Communion while that situation lasts. Divorced Catholics who have not remarried can receive the sacraments, and a spouse who separates to protect themselves or their children commits no sin (canons 1151–1155).

CCC 1625–1629, 1650, 2382–2386 · CIC can. 1056, 1141, 1151–1155, 1671

Why does the Church reject sex before marriage, living together and contraception?

Sex belongs in marriage. It unites spouses and is open to life. Sex outside marriage and contraception split these apart. Couples may space births with natural family planning.

Two purposes. Sex belongs in marriage and has two purposes that belong together: it unites husband and wife, and it is open to new life. With the body it says, “I give you my whole life.” Outside marriage the body says what the couple has not yet promised, so the Church calls sex before marriage a grave sin. Couples living together who want to marry in the Church are welcome: talk to your priest. Many such couples choose to live chastely until the wedding.

Contraception separates the two purposes by making a sexual act unable to give life. This doesn’t mean having as many children as possible. For serious reasons, a couple may space births or avoid pregnancy with natural family planning (NFP): they learn the wife’s fertile days and don’t have sex on those days. Contraception changes the act itself; NFP leaves each act whole and only chooses when. Ask your parish about an NFP class.

CCC 2353, 2366–2372, 2390–2391, 2399

Why does the Church reject IVF and surrogacy?

A child is a gift, not a product. IVF conceives apart from the parents’ act of love and usually destroys embryos. Surrogacy uses the mother and treats the baby as goods. Every child born this way is still fully loved.

IVF. It makes a child in a laboratory, apart from the parents’ act of love. A child is a gift, never something owed, and has the right “to be the fruit of the specific act of the conjugal love of his parents” (CCC 2378). IVF also usually creates extra embryos that are frozen or destroyed, and each one is a human life.

A child born through IVF is fully loved by God and has the same dignity as any other child. But that does not make the method right.

Surrogacy goes further: a woman carries a child for others and hands the baby over after birth, usually for money. The child is treated as a product and the mother’s body as a service. In January 2024 Pope Francis called surrogacy “deplorable” and “a grave violation of the dignity of the woman and the child, based on the exploitation of situations of the mother’s material needs,” and asked for a worldwide ban. The Church’s 2024 document Dignitas Infinita repeats this.

The Church supports fertility treatment that helps a couple conceive through their own act of love, such as NaProTechnology.

CCC 2373–2379

Why does the Church say masturbation is wrong?

It is gravely wrong, but guilt can be lessened. Sex is meant as a gift to a spouse. If you struggle, don’t lose heart: confess, pray and ask for help.

Why it is wrong. Masturbation seeks sexual pleasure alone, apart from the gift of oneself to a spouse. The Church calls it “gravely disordered” (CCC 2352), but also says habit, anxiety, immaturity and other factors can lessen a person’s guilt. If you struggle with it, don’t lose heart: go to confession often, pray, and talk to a priest. Many people find freedom over time.

Follow-up: What if a doctor asks me for a sperm sample for fertility tests?

Fertility testing is good, but giving the sample by masturbation is not morally acceptable. There is a moral way: a special collection sheath, perforated and without spermicide, used during normal intercourse between husband and wife. Ask your doctor for one. Doctors trained in NaProTechnology or other Catholic-friendly fertility care know it well, and your priest can help with questions.

CCC 2352

How should we treat people who struggle with same-sex attraction or their sexual identity?

With love first. Every person is made in God’s image and deserves respect. Having these feelings is not a sin.

Respect and closeness. Every person is made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27). The Church asks that people with same-sex attraction be treated with “respect, compassion, and sensitivity,” without unjust discrimination. Having these feelings or struggles is not a sin; most people don’t choose them, and many carry deep pain. Our first job is to stay close, listen and pray.

The Church also teaches that body and soul belong together, and being a man or a woman is a gift from God. Dignitas Infinita (2024) warns that sex-change treatments risk harming that God-given dignity. Everyone outside marriage between a man and a woman is called to chastity. The Church calls homosexual acts gravely wrong, never the person.

You can welcome someone and be kind to them without agreeing with everything they believe. For hard family situations, talk with your priest.

CCC 357, 364–365, 2332–2333, 2357–2359

Which baptisms does the Church accept, and are Mormons Christian?

Trinitarian baptisms with water. Orthodox and most Protestant baptisms are valid. Mormon baptisms are not, because they deny that Jesus is eternal God.

What makes a baptism valid. A baptism is valid with real water, the words “I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit,” and the intention to do what the Church does (canon 849). So the Church accepts Orthodox baptisms and those of most Protestant churches (Anglican, Lutheran, Presbyterian, Methodist, Baptist). These Christians are not baptized again (canon 845); they are received into full communion.

A baptism is invalid without water (Quakers, the Salvation Army, Christian Science) or when the words deny the Trinity (Jehovah’s Witnesses, Unitarians, “Jesus only” Pentecostals, Mormons). In 2001 the Church declared Mormon baptisms invalid: Mormons use the right words but believe Jesus is a created spirit child of the Father, not God from all eternity. So they are not Christians in the Church’s sense, though they are often devout people we treat with respect.

If you are unsure about your own baptism, your priest or catechist will check your record and, if needed, arrange a conditional or full baptism (canon 869).

CCC 232–234, 1239–1240, 1256 · CIC can. 845, 849, 869

We married before either of us was baptized. What happens to our marriage?

Your marriage is already valid. A marriage of two unbaptized people is a valid natural marriage. Once you are both baptized, it becomes a sacrament with no extra ceremony.

No convalidation needed. The Church sees a marriage between two unbaptized people as a valid natural marriage, even if it was only a civil wedding. It does not need convalidation and is no obstacle to receiving the sacraments through OCIA. Convalidation is only for marriages the Church considers invalid, for example when a Catholic married outside the Church, or married an unbaptized person without a dispensation (canon 1086).

Once you are both baptized, your marriage becomes a sacrament automatically (canon 1055 §2). If one spouse is baptized at Easter and the other later, it becomes a sacrament at the second baptism. No extra ceremony is needed.

CCC 1601, 1617, 1631 · CIC can. 1055 §2, 1086

Why does the Church oppose abortion?

Every human life is sacred. Life has full worth from conception. Directly ending an innocent life is always gravely wrong.

Life from conception. Every human life is a gift from God and has full worth from conception to natural death. Directly ending an innocent life is always gravely wrong, whatever the motive. Christians have taught this about abortion since the first century.

From conception there is a new human being with its own DNA, growing as each of us once grew. The hard cases are real: rape, poverty, fear, a frightening diagnosis. But how a child was conceived or how sick the child is does not change who the child is. Two lives are involved, and the Church asks us to care for both, with real help for mothers in need.

Treating a serious illness in the mother is allowed even if the baby may die as a side effect, as long as the baby’s death is not the aim.

Procuring an abortion brings automatic excommunication (canon 1397 §2), yet anyone who has had or taken part in one can be forgiven in confession, and priests will receive you with mercy.

CCC 2270–2275 · CIC can. 1397 §2

Why does the Church oppose euthanasia and assisted suicide?

It is killing, whatever the motive. A patient’s request or the law does not change that. Each life keeps its worth to its natural end.

Mercy or killing? Ending a sick person’s life to end their suffering, or helping them end it (in Canada, MAID), is killing. The patient’s request or the law’s permission does not change that. Each life belongs to God and keeps its worth to its natural end.

You don’t have to keep someone alive at any cost. A patient may refuse treatment that is too burdensome or out of proportion, and strong painkillers are allowed even if they may shorten life, as long as the aim is to relieve pain. Basic care such as food, water and comfort is still owed. The Church’s answer to suffering is good palliative care and never leaving the sick alone.

The Church does not give up hope for people who take their own lives. Fear, depression or great pain can lessen their responsibility, and we pray for them.

CCC 2276–2283

Why did the Church change its teaching on the death penalty?

It is now “inadmissible.” In 2018 the Catechism was revised: the death penalty attacks human dignity, and the Church works to end it.

The 2018 change. Pope Francis revised the Catechism. It now says that, in the light of the Gospel, the death penalty is “inadmissible because it is an attack on the inviolability and dignity of the person,” and that the Church works to end it worldwide.

In the past, the Church accepted the death penalty in very serious cases, when it was the only way to protect people from a dangerous criminal. In 1995 John Paul II wrote that such cases were “very rare, if not practically non-existent,” because modern prisons can keep society safe.

The principles stay the same: every human life has dignity, and society may protect itself. What changed is the judgment that killing is still needed for that, along with a clearer sense that even a guilty person keeps his dignity.

CCC 2266–2267

Why can’t women be ordained priests?

The Church has no authority to. Jesus chose only men as apostles, though He broke many customs about women. This teaching is final.

Christ’s choice. The Church teaches that it has no authority to ordain women (canon 1024). Jesus chose only men as His twelve apostles, and the apostles also chose men to succeed them. Jesus broke many customs of His day about women: He spoke with them in public, taught them and had women among His closest followers. So His choice of men was not just following the culture. In 1994 John Paul II declared that all the faithful must hold this teaching as final.

This says nothing against the worth of women. Men and women are equal in dignity before God. Mary, the greatest of all saints, was never a priest. Women serve the Church as teachers, catechists, sisters, missionaries and leaders, and Catherine of Siena and Teresa of Avila are Doctors of the Church, a title given to its greatest teachers.

The priesthood is not a higher rank, a prize or an honor, and no one has a right to it. Jesus told His apostles: “Whoever wishes to become great among you must be your servant… for the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve” (Mark 10:43–45). He washed their feet and told them to do the same (John 13:14–15). The Catechism says the priest’s office “is in the strict sense of the term a service.”

CCC 876, 1551, 1577–1578 · CIC can. 1024

Why can’t priests get married?

Celibacy is a rule, not a doctrine. Latin-rite priests don’t marry, but Eastern Catholic Churches ordain married men.

Who may be ordained. Celibacy is a Church rule (canon 277 §1), not a doctrine, and it could change. In the Latin Church a married man is normally barred from becoming a priest (canon 1042), but the Eastern Catholic Churches ordain married men, and some married Protestant ministers who became Catholic have been ordained with a dispensation. A man who is already a priest cannot marry (canon 1087).

The Latin Church keeps the rule because Jesus never married and praised those who give up marriage “for the sake of the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 19:12). An unmarried man can give his full attention to the Lord (1 Corinthians 7:32–33) and his whole life to his people. His life also points to heaven, where people “neither marry nor are given in marriage” (Matthew 22:30).

CCC 1579–1580 · CIC can. 277, 1042, 1087

Why baptize babies who can’t choose for themselves?

Grace is a gift, not a choice we earn. Children need God’s grace too, so parents give it early, as they give food and medicine.

A gift, not a reward. Baptism is a free gift from God that nobody earns, adults included. Children are born with original sin and need God’s grace like everyone else. Parents don’t wait for a child to choose food or medicine, and the Church asks them to have the child baptized in the first few weeks (canon 867 §1).

The practice is very old: the New Testament tells of whole households being baptized (Acts 16:15, 33; 1 Corinthians 1:16). The child still says yes later, growing into the faith that parents and godparents promise to teach (canon 872), especially at Confirmation.

CCC 1250–1252 · CIC can. 867, 872–874

What does the Church teach about Mary: was she sinless, ever-virgin, taken into heaven?

Yes, all three. Mary was kept free from sin from conception, stayed a virgin all her life, and was taken body and soul into heaven.

Sinless. From the first moment of her life, God kept Mary free from original sin (the Immaculate Conception, defined in 1854). She was saved by Jesus like us, but in advance. The angel greets her as “full of grace” (Luke 1:28). “All have sinned” (Romans 3:23) speaks of people in general; it does not include Jesus either, or a baby who has done nothing.

Taken into heaven. At the end of her life Mary was taken body and soul into heaven (the Assumption, defined in 1950), the first to share fully in her Son’s Resurrection. The Bible does not describe it, but Christians believed it from early on, and no city ever claimed to have her body.

Ever-virgin. Mary remained a virgin all her life, and Jesus is her only son. The Gospels name “brothers” of Jesus (Mark 6:3), but Hebrew and Aramaic had no word for “cousin,” so “brother” was used for close relatives; Lot, Abraham’s nephew, is called his “brother” (Genesis 14:14). James and Joses were sons of another Mary (Matthew 27:56). At the cross Jesus gave His mother to John’s care (John 19:26–27), which makes little sense if she had other sons.

CCC 490–493, 499–501, 966

Are we saved by faith alone, or by works?

By grace, through a living faith. Salvation is God’s free gift. True faith shows itself in works, but even those works are His grace in us.

Grace first. We are saved by God’s grace, a free gift we cannot earn. The first grace, being made right with God, comes through faith and baptism, and no one can merit it. Catholics and many Protestants agree on this; in 1999 the Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation signed a joint statement on it.

Saving faith is a living faith. “Faith by itself, if it has no works, is dead” (James 2:17). The only time the Bible says “faith alone” is: “a person is justified by works and not by faith alone” (James 2:24). Paul speaks of “faith working through love” (Galatians 5:6).

Good works do not earn heaven. God’s grace comes first and works in us, and we respond by doing good. So even our good works are His gift.

CCC 1987–1996, 2006–2011

Why is the God of the Old Testament so violent?

God revealed Himself step by step. He met a violent people where they were. The Bible is read as a whole with Christ as its key, and ancient war stories often exaggerate.

Read as one whole. The Old Testament has hard passages, and the Church doesn’t hide them. It reads the Bible as one whole, with Christ as the key. God revealed Himself step by step to a people in a violent age, starting where they were. The Catechism says these books “contain matters imperfect and provisional.” The writers also used the styles of their time, and ancient war stories often exaggerated: Joshua says whole peoples were wiped out, and a few chapters later they are still there (Joshua 10; Judges 1).

Jesus shows us fully who God is: “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father” (John 14:9). He taught us to love our enemies and died for His. We read the hard passages in His light.

CCC 110, 121–123, 128–130

Can Catholics believe in reincarnation, or be cremated?

Reincarnation: no. Cremation: yes. We live once and rise in our own bodies. Cremation is allowed, but the ashes belong in a sacred place, not scattered or kept at home.

Reincarnation: no. We each live one life on earth. “It is appointed for mortals to die once, and after that the judgment” (Hebrews 9:27). The Catechism says plainly: “There is no ‘reincarnation’ after death.” At the end of time we will rise in our own bodies.

Cremation: yes, unless it is chosen to deny faith in the resurrection, although the Church prefers burial of the body (canon 1176 §3). The ashes are to be kept in a sacred place such as a cemetery. They should not be scattered or kept at home (Ad resurgendum cum Christo, 2016).

CCC 1013, 2300–2301 · CIC can. 1176 §3

Do Catholics believe in angels, demons and exorcism? What about horoscopes and tarot?

Angels and demons are real; the occult is off limits. Christ has already defeated Satan, and exorcism is done only with the bishop’s permission. Horoscopes, tarot and mediums turn us away from God.

Angels and demons. Both are real. God created angels as spiritual beings, and each of us has a guardian angel (Matthew 18:10). Some angels freely rejected God and became demons, led by Satan. Jesus drove out demons many times. Satan is real but only a creature, not God’s equal, and Christ has already defeated him.

Exorcism. Every baptism includes a simple prayer of exorcism. A full exorcism may be done only by a priest with the bishop’s permission (canon 1172), after doctors have ruled out mental or physical illness. True possession is rare.

Horoscopes, tarot, fortune-telling, mediums and Ouija boards must all be rejected. They try to know or control the future without God, or turn to powers other than God. Even “just for fun,” they teach us to look for guidance in the wrong place and can do real spiritual harm. Trust God, who holds the future. If you have used these things, mention it in confession and get rid of tarot cards, charms and the like.
