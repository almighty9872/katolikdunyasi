Parables of the Kingdom

God’s Kingdom starts small, grows quietly and is worth everything you have.

The Sower

God’s word only bears fruit in a heart that truly receives it.Matthew 13:1–23 · Mark 4:1–20 · Luke 8:4–15

The story

A farmer's seed is eaten by birds, withers on rocky ground and is choked by thorns; the seed that falls on good soil yields thirty, sixty and a hundredfold.

Why Jesus told it

Jesus is teaching from a boat to a huge crowd on the shore of the Sea of Galilee. Many are drawn to Him, but few will follow Him to the end. Later, alone with the disciples, He explains the parable Himself, one of the few times He does so.

The key

The sower
Christ, and everyone who passes on His word
The seed
the word of God
The path
a heart that hears but does not understand, so the evil one snatches the word away
Rocky ground
enthusiasm without roots, which gives up when trouble comes
Thorns
the cares of the world and the lure of riches that choke the word
Good soil
a heart that hears, understands and bears fruit

What it means

The sower is surprisingly generous: he throws seed everywhere, even where it seems wasted. God offers His word to everyone, with no one written off in advance.

The difference lies in the soil. Most of us recognize all four soils in ourselves on different days: distracted, eager then discouraged, crowded with worries. The parable is less a verdict than an invitation to clear the ground. Soil can be tilled; stones can be removed; thorns can be pulled up. A heart that keeps making room for the word will in time bear fruit far beyond its own effort.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism says the parables are like mirrors: in them we ask whether we are hard soil or good earth. Hearing is not enough; deeds are required.

CCC 546

For your life

  • Which of the four soils best describes how I received God’s word this week?
  • What is one “thorn” I could pull up to give the word more room?

The Wheat and the Weeds

Good and evil grow together until the harvest; judgment belongs to God.Matthew 13:24–30, 36–43

The story

While a man sleeps, an enemy sows weeds among his wheat. He won't let his servants pull them up in case the wheat comes up with them; the sorting can wait for the harvest.

Why Jesus told it

This follows straight after the Sower in Matthew 13. The first Christians were puzzled: if the Kingdom has come, why is there still so much evil, even among believers? Jesus later explains the parable to His disciples in private (Matthew 13:36–43).

The key

The man who sows good seed
the Son of Man
The field
the world
The good seed
the children of the Kingdom
The weeds
the children of the evil one
The enemy
the devil
The harvest
the end of the age, when the angels will gather and sort

What it means

The weed in question, darnel, looks almost identical to wheat while young. Pulling it up early would tear out good plants with it. The servants are eager to purify the field; the master is patient.

This is a warning against the temptation to build a “pure” community by our own hand, deciding who is beyond hope. People change: today’s weed can become tomorrow’s saint, as St Paul and St Augustine show. Evil is real and will be judged, but the sorting is God’s work, done at the right time. Our task in the meantime is to be good wheat.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism applies this to the Church herself: until the end of time, the weeds of sin are found mixed with the good wheat of the Gospel in all her members.

CCC 827

For your life

  • Is there someone I have quietly written off as a “weed”?
  • Where in my own heart do wheat and weeds grow side by side?

The Mustard Seed

God’s Kingdom starts tiny and grows beyond anything we imagine.Matthew 13:31–32 · Mark 4:30–32 · Luke 13:18–19

The story

The mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, grows into a tree where birds build their nests.

Why Jesus told it

The disciples were a handful of fishermen and tax collectors in a small province of the Roman Empire. Expectations for the Messiah were grand and political. Jesus answers with an image from the kitchen garden.

The key

The mustard seed
the Kingdom in its beginning: Jesus and His little band of disciples
The great plant
the Church spread through the world
The birds that nest in its branches
the nations who find shelter in it

What it means

Twelve men, a crucified leader, an empty tomb: from the outside, Christianity began as almost nothing. Within three centuries it filled the Roman world, and today it reaches every continent.

The same pattern holds in a single life. God rarely starts with something impressive. A short prayer, a small act of kindness, a first step back to Mass can be the mustard seed. We are not asked to produce the tree, only to plant the seed and trust God with the growth.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism calls the “little flock” gathered around Jesus the seed and beginning of the Kingdom, which God makes grow.

CCC 764

For your life

  • What small seed of faith has God planted in me that I have overlooked?
  • Do I despise small beginnings, in myself or in others?

The Leaven

The Kingdom changes the world quietly, from the inside out.Matthew 13:33 · Luke 13:20–21

The story

A woman mixes a little yeast into a large batch of flour, and the whole dough rises.

Why Jesus told it

Told as a pair with the Mustard Seed. One is a man’s work in the field, the other a woman’s work in the kitchen: the Kingdom touches every part of life.

The key

The leaven (yeast)
the Kingdom, the Gospel, the grace of God
Three measures of flour
the world, or the whole of a person’s life
The woman
Christ, or the Church, working the Kingdom into the world

What it means

Three measures of flour is roughly 40 liters, enough bread for a large crowd. A pinch of yeast hidden in it changes all of it, silently and completely.

Christians are not meant to stay apart in a sealed jar. Like yeast, they are mixed into families, offices and neighborhoods, and the change happens there. It is slow and mostly unseen. The same is true inside us: grace does not usually transform us overnight, but works through every corner of our lives until “till it was all leavened.”

What the Church teaches

The Catechism, explaining the Our Father, says that the newness of the Kingdom should make the earth “rise” like leaven in the dough, through the Spirit of Christ.

CCC 2832

For your life

  • Where has God placed me to be “yeast” right now?
  • Which part of my life have I kept sealed off from grace?

The Hidden Treasure

The Kingdom is worth everything, and finding it brings joy.Matthew 13:44

The story

A man finds treasure buried in a field and, overjoyed, sells everything he owns to buy the field.

Why Jesus told it

In a land often crossed by armies, people buried their valuables for safekeeping. Treasures were sometimes found centuries later by a farmhand working someone else’s field.

The key

The treasure
the Kingdom of God, and Christ Himself
The field
the place where we stumble on it, often in ordinary life
Selling everything
a whole-hearted response that holds nothing back

What it means

The key word is joy. The man is not grimly making a sacrifice; he is a man who has just found a fortune and can’t believe his luck. Selling everything is the obvious, happy thing to do.

Saints often describe their conversion this way. What looks like giving things up from the outside feels, from the inside, like gaining something far greater. If faith feels only like a list of losses, we may not yet have seen the treasure clearly.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism says that through His parables Jesus asks for a radical choice: to gain the Kingdom, one must give everything.

CCC 546

For your life

  • When did I last feel joy, not just duty, in my faith?
  • What am I holding on to that I value more than the treasure?

The Pearl of Great Price

For the one who searches, Christ is the pearl worth more than all else.Matthew 13:45–46

The story

A merchant looking for fine pearls finds one of great value and sells everything to buy it.

Why Jesus told it

A twin to the Hidden Treasure. In the ancient world pearls were among the most precious things anyone could own, more valuable than gold.

The key

The merchant
the seeker who is looking for truth and goodness
Fine pearls
the good things of life: wisdom, beauty, love
The one great pearl
Christ and His Kingdom, which fulfill all the others

What it means

The man with the treasure stumbled on it; the merchant was searching. Some meet God by surprise, others after a long quest through philosophies and religions. Both are welcome.

The merchant already deals in fine pearls. The good things he loved were real, but they pointed beyond themselves. When he finds the one pearl, everything else is reordered around it. Faith does not throw away beauty, learning or love; it finds their source.

What the Church teaches

The same Catechism passage applies: the Kingdom asks for a radical choice, giving everything to gain it.

CCC 546

For your life

  • What “fine pearls” am I seeking, and where do they point?
  • Is Christ the pearl at the center of my life, or one among many?

The Net

The net gathers everyone now; the sorting comes at the end.Matthew 13:47–50

The story

A net thrown into the sea gathers fish of every kind; the fishermen keep the good ones and throw the bad away.

Why Jesus told it

Several of the apostles were fishermen on the Sea of Galilee who used large dragnets. Jesus closes His chapter of Kingdom parables with an image they knew from their own work.

The key

The net
the Kingdom, and the Church in the world
Fish of every kind
all kinds of people drawn in by the Gospel
Sorting on the shore
the final judgment, carried out by the angels

What it means

The net does not choose: it pulls in every kind of fish. The Church’s mission is the same, casting the net wide to everyone without picking who seems worthy.

But the story does not end in the water. On the shore there is a sorting, and it is serious. Being in the net, a member of the Church in name, is not the same as being good fish. The parable calls us to take our own conversion seriously while leaving the judgment of others to God.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism teaches that at the Last Judgment the truth of each person’s relationship with God will be laid bare, and Jesus speaks plainly of the danger of being lost.

CCC 1038, 1034

For your life

  • Do I treat belonging to the Church as a guarantee, or as a calling?
  • How would I live today if the net were drawn ashore tonight?

The Growing Seed

We sow; God gives the growth, in His own time.Mark 4:26–29

The story

A man scatters seed; whether he sleeps or wakes, it sprouts and grows, and he doesn’t know how.

Why Jesus told it

This short parable is found only in Mark. It sits between the Sower and the Mustard Seed, and it was a comfort to early missionaries who saw little result from their work.

The key

The man
the disciple who spreads the word
The seed
the word of God
Growth while he sleeps
God’s hidden work, which we cannot see or control
The harvest
the full coming of the Kingdom

What it means

The farmer does his part and then goes to bed. He cannot make the seed grow faster by worrying over it. The earth bears fruit “of itself”.

This frees us from two errors: laziness (doing nothing) and anxiety (thinking everything depends on us). Parents praying for children who have left the faith, catechists who see no results, anyone working for good with little to show: the seed is growing, even when nothing seems to happen.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism describes the Kingdom as present in the word of Christ, compared to a seed sown in a field, growing by its own power until the harvest.

CCC 764

For your life

  • Where am I trying to force growth that only God can give?
  • Whose slow growth am I tempted to give up on?

Parables of Mercy and Forgiveness

God’s mercy has no limit, and He expects us to pass it on.

The Lost Sheep

God goes looking for the one who is lost, and rejoices to find Him.Luke 15:3–7 · Matthew 18:12–14

The story

A shepherd with a hundred sheep leaves the ninety-nine to search for the one that is lost, and carries it home on his shoulders with joy.

Why Jesus told it

Tax collectors and sinners were gathering to hear Jesus, and the Pharisees and scribes grumbled: “This man receives sinners and eats with them.” Jesus answers with three stories in a row: the lost sheep, the lost coin and the lost son.

The key

The shepherd
God, and Jesus the Good Shepherd
The lost sheep
a sinner who has wandered away
The ninety-nine
those who think they have no need of repentance
Carrying it home
God does the work of bringing us back
The celebration
the joy of heaven over one who repents

What it means

Leaving ninety-nine sheep to look for one seems reckless. That is the point: God’s love is not careful accounting. Every single person matters to Him as if they were the only one.

Notice that the shepherd does not drive the sheep home or scold it; he lifts it onto his shoulders. The early Christians loved this image and painted it in the catacombs long before they painted the cross. Whoever feels too far gone should know that God is already out looking.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism notes that Jesus invites sinners to the table of the Kingdom and speaks of the Father’s joy over even one sinner who repents.

CCC 545

For your life

  • Have I ever felt like the lost sheep? How did God find me?
  • Who in my life is “lost”, and how can I go after them?

The Lost Coin

Each person is precious to God, worth searching the whole house for.Luke 15:8–10

The story

A woman with ten silver coins loses one, lights a lamp and sweeps the house until she finds it, then celebrates with her neighbors.

Why Jesus told it

The second of the three “lost” parables of Luke 15, told to the same grumbling listeners. This time the searcher is a woman, which would have surprised Jesus’ audience.

The key

The woman
God, searching with patient care
The lost coin
a soul that has gone missing
The lamp and broom
the light and effort God spends to find us
The friends and neighbors
the angels, who share the joy

What it means

A silver drachma was about a day’s wage. For a poor household, losing one was a real loss, and it may have been part of a woman’s dowry. The search is thorough: she lights a lamp and sweeps every corner.

A sheep wanders off on its own, but a coin is simply lost; it cannot even know it is missing. Some people are far from God not through rebellion but through neglect, wounds or circumstance. God searches for them too, and the joy of finding is shared with all heaven.

What the Church teaches

With the lost sheep, this parable shows what the Catechism calls Jesus’ invitation to sinners, and heaven’s joy over each one who returns.

CCC 545

For your life

  • Do I see myself as precious to God, or as easily replaced?
  • Who around me is lost without even knowing it?

The Prodigal Son

The Father runs to welcome the sinner home, and longs for both His sons.Luke 15:11–32

The story

The younger son takes his inheritance, squanders it far away and ends up feeding pigs. When he decides to come home as a hired hand, his father sees him from afar, runs to him, embraces him and throws a feast. The elder son is angry and won't go in, and the father comes out to plead with him too.

Why Jesus told it

The third and longest story in Luke 15, aimed at the same Pharisees and scribes who resented Jesus eating with sinners. They would have seen themselves, uncomfortably, in the elder son.

The key

The father
God the Father, rich in mercy
The younger son
the sinner who leaves God and comes back
The far country and the pigs
the emptiness and degradation of life without God
The robe, ring and shoes
restored dignity as a son, not a servant
The elder son
the religious person who obeys but does not share the Father’s heart

What it means

Asking for his inheritance early was like telling his father, “I wish you were dead.” Yet the father never stops watching the road. When he sees his son, he runs, something a dignified elder of the time would never do, and cuts short the speech the son had rehearsed. The son hoped to come back as a servant; he is restored as a son.

The story has two halves. The elder son has never left home, but he is just as far from his father’s heart. He calls himself a slave and his brother “this son of yours”. The father goes out to him too, with the same tenderness. Jesus ends without telling us whether he goes in to the feast. The question is left for each listener: am I willing to rejoice when God forgives someone I think does not deserve it?

What the Church teaches

The Catechism uses this parable to describe the whole process of conversion and repentance: the fascination of illusory freedom, the misery, the decision to return, the father’s welcome and the festive joy, which only the heart of Christ could reveal so simply and beautifully.

CCC 1439

For your life

  • Am I more like the younger son or the elder son right now?
  • Is there a “rehearsed speech” keeping me from going to Confession?

The Unforgiving Servant

Having been forgiven so much, we must forgive others from the heart.Matthew 18:23–35

The story

A king forgives a servant a debt too great ever to repay. That same servant has a fellow servant thrown into prison over a small debt, and the king takes back his pardon.

Why Jesus told it

Peter has just asked: “Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? As many as seven times?” Jesus answers “seventy times seven”, then tells this story to show why.

The key

The king
God
Ten thousand talents
our debt of sin, impossible to repay
The first servant
each of us, forgiven by God
A hundred denarii
the real but small wrongs others do to us
The torturers
the consequence of a heart that refuses mercy

What it means

The numbers are deliberately absurd. Ten thousand talents was more than the tax revenue of a whole province, a debt no servant could repay in a hundred lifetimes. A hundred denarii was about three months’ wages: a real debt, but tiny by comparison.

The servant’s fault is not that he wanted his money; it is that he forgot what had just been done for him. Forgiveness received but not passed on has not really been received. This is why we pray, “forgive us our trespasses as we forgive those who trespass against us.” Forgiving does not mean pretending the wrong did not happen; it means releasing the debt, as God released ours.

What the Church teaches

Explaining the Our Father, the Catechism points to this parable: it is in the depths of the heart that everything is bound and loosed, and our openness to God’s mercy depends on our forgiving others.

CCC 2843

For your life

  • Whose “hundred denarii” am I still holding on to?
  • Do I remember often enough how much I have been forgiven?

The Pharisee and the Tax Collector

God hears the humble who ask for mercy, not the proud who list their virtues.Luke 18:9–14

The story

In the Temple a Pharisee lists his virtues and thanks God he isn't like others. A tax collector, not daring to look up, beats his breast: "God, be merciful to me, a sinner."

Why Jesus told it

Luke says Jesus told this “to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and despised others.” It follows straight after the Persistent Widow, so it is also a lesson on how to pray.

The key

The Pharisee
a person confident in his own goodness
The tax collector
a sinner who knows he has nothing to offer God
The Temple
the place of prayer, where both stand before the same God
Going home justified
being made right with God, as a gift

What it means

Pharisees were respected for their devotion, and this one really did fast and tithe more than the law required. Tax collectors worked for the Roman occupiers and often cheated their own people. Jesus’ listeners would have expected the verdict to go the other way.

The Pharisee’s prayer is really about himself, and about how he compares with others. The tax collector stands at a distance, eyes down, and says a single sentence. He hides nothing and asks for everything. The Church has made his prayer her own: it lives on in the Kyrie at Mass and in the Jesus Prayer, “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner.”

What the Church teaches

The Catechism names this as one of Luke’s three great parables on prayer. It teaches that humility is the foundation of prayer and that petition begins, like the tax collector’s, with asking forgiveness.

CCC 2613, 2631, 2559

For your life

  • When I pray, do I compare myself with others?
  • Can I pray the tax collector’s prayer slowly today, meaning every word?

The Two Debtors

The one who knows how much they are forgiven loves the most.Luke 7:41–43

The story

A creditor cancels the debts of two men, one owing five hundred denarii, the other fifty. Jesus asks His host, Simon the Pharisee: which will love him more?

Why Jesus told it

Jesus is dining at the house of Simon, a Pharisee. A woman known in the town as a sinner comes in, weeps over Jesus’ feet, dries them with her hair and anoints them. Simon thinks to himself that a real prophet would know what sort of woman she is. Jesus answers his thought with this story.

The key

The creditor
God, who forgives freely
The debtor of five hundred
the sinful woman, aware of how much she has been forgiven
The debtor of fifty
Simon, who thinks he owes little
Cancelling the debts
God’s free forgiveness, which neither could earn

What it means

Five hundred denarii was well over a year’s wages; fifty, about two months. Neither man could pay, and the creditor forgives both. Simon gives the obvious answer: the one forgiven more will love more.

Jesus then turns to the woman and contrasts her extravagant love with Simon’s cool hospitality: no water for His feet, no kiss, no oil. The difference is not that Simon is sinless. It is that he does not see his need. The saints often called themselves the greatest of sinners, not out of false modesty, but because the closer they came to God, the more they saw how much they had been forgiven, and the more they loved.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism describes contemplative prayer as the prayer of the forgiven sinner who welcomes the love by which he is loved and wants to respond by loving even more, pointing to this scene.

CCC 2712

For your life

  • Do I think of myself as someone who owes God fifty or five hundred?
  • How does gratitude for forgiveness show in the way I love?

The Barren Fig Tree

God is patient and gives us time to bear fruit, but the time is not endless.Luke 13:6–9

The story

A fig tree that has borne no fruit for three years is about to be cut down; the gardener asks for one more year to dig around it and fertilize it.

Why Jesus told it

People had just told Jesus about Galileans killed by Pilate, and He recalled eighteen people crushed when a tower fell. Were they worse sinners than others? “No,” He says, “but unless you repent you will all likewise perish.” Then He tells this parable.

The key

The owner of the vineyard
God the Father
The fig tree
God’s people, and each of us
Three years without fruit
a life that has received much but given little back
The vinedresser
Christ, who intercedes for us
Digging and manure
the graces God gives to help us change

What it means

A fig tree takes up space, water and nutrients. After three barren years, cutting it down is common sense. The vinedresser pleads for one more year and promises to work on it himself.

This is the season we live in: the extra year. Christ stands between us and judgment, not only pleading but digging and feeding the roots with grace. The parable is both a comfort and a warning. God’s patience is great, but it is meant to lead us to repentance, not to be presumed upon. The story ends before we learn whether the tree bears fruit. That ending is ours to write.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism calls conversion first of all a work of the grace of God, who makes our hearts return to Him.

CCC 1432

For your life

  • What fruit is God looking for in my life this year?
  • How am I using the “extra year” I have been given?

The Good Samaritan

My neighbor is anyone in need, and love crosses every boundary.Luke 10:25–37

The story

A man is robbed, beaten and left half dead by the road. A priest and a Levite pass by on the other side; a Samaritan, from a people the Jews despised, binds his wounds, takes him to an inn and pays for his care.

Why Jesus told it

A lawyer asks Jesus what he must do to inherit eternal life. He answers correctly: love God and love your neighbor. Then, “desiring to justify himself”, he asks: “And who is my neighbor?” This story is Jesus’ reply.

The key

The traveler
every person in need, and humanity wounded by sin
The priest and the Levite
religion that keeps its distance from suffering
The Samaritan
the unexpected neighbor, and in the Fathers’ reading, Christ Himself
Oil and wine
healing care, and for the Fathers, the sacraments
The inn
the Church, where the wounded are cared for until Christ returns

What it means

The road from Jerusalem down to Jericho drops about a kilometer in 27 kilometers through desert, and was notorious for bandits. The priest and Levite may have feared ritual impurity from touching a dead body. Samaritans and Jews despised each other; for Jesus’ audience, “good” and “Samaritan” did not belong together.

Notice how Jesus flips the question. The lawyer asked, “Who is my neighbor?”, looking for the limit of his duty. Jesus asks, “Which of these was neighbor to the man?” The question is not who deserves my love, but whether I will become a neighbor to whoever is in front of me. The Church Fathers, such as St Augustine, also saw Christ as the Samaritan who came down to wounded humanity, healed us and paid the cost of our care.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism teaches that charity asks us to love as Christ loves, even our enemies, and to make ourselves the neighbor of those farthest away. The works of mercy are its concrete form.

CCC 1825, 2447

For your life

  • Who have I “passed by on the other side” recently?
  • Is there a group of people I find it hard to see as my neighbor?

Parables of Prayer and Perseverance

Two short stories about praying with trust, and not giving up.

The Persistent Widow

Keep praying and never lose heart: God hears His children.Luke 18:1–8

The story

A judge who neither fears God nor respects people finally gives a persistent widow her rights, just so she'll stop bothering him.

Why Jesus told it

Luke tells us the purpose before the story: Jesus told it “that they ought always to pray and not lose heart.” It comes after His teaching on the day the Son of Man will be revealed, and ends with a question: “when the Son of man comes, will he find faith on earth?”

The key

The unjust judge
not a picture of God, but His opposite
The widow
those who are powerless and depend on God alone
Her persistence
prayer that keeps going when nothing seems to change
Justice at last
God’s sure answer, in His time

What it means

In Jesus’ time a widow had no husband to speak for her in court and often no money to bribe a judge. Her only weapon is to keep coming back. The judge gives in purely to get rid of her.

The parable argues from the lesser to the greater. If a corrupt judge gives in to persistence, how much more will a loving Father hear His children who cry to Him day and night? Persistence in prayer does not wear God down; it changes us. It deepens our desire, purifies what we ask for and keeps our faith alive while we wait. St Monica prayed for her son Augustine for some seventeen years.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism lists this among the three principal parables on prayer in Luke, teaching the patience of faith: we must pray always without losing heart.

CCC 2613

For your life

  • What have I stopped praying for because the answer seemed slow?
  • Does my prayer show that I trust God more than the widow trusted the judge?

The Friend at Midnight

Ask boldly and keep knocking: God loves to give good things.Luke 11:5–8

The story

A man with an unexpected midnight guest knocks on his neighbor's door to borrow bread. The neighbor doesn't want to get up, but gives him what he needs because he keeps knocking.

Why Jesus told it

The disciples have just asked, “Lord, teach us to pray,” and Jesus has given them the Our Father. This parable follows immediately, and then: “Ask, and it will be given you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you.”

The key

The man knocking
each of us at prayer
The friend in bed
not God, who never sleeps, but a contrast to Him
The three loaves
what we need, for ourselves and for others
The unexpected guest

What it means

Hospitality was a sacred duty, and a traveler arriving at midnight could not be turned away hungry. Villages baked bread daily, so everyone knew who might have some left. The whole family sleeps in one room; getting up means waking everyone.

The man is not asking for himself but for a guest. Much of our prayer is like this: we go to God because someone else needs bread. Jesus encourages a holy boldness, even a kind of shamelessness, in asking. And Luke ends the passage with the best gift of all: the Father will give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism names this as the first of Luke’s three great parables on prayer, inviting urgent, persistent prayer: “Knock, and it will be opened to you.”

CCC 2613

For your life

  • For whom do I need to go and knock today?
  • Do I ask God boldly, or do I hold back as if bothering Him?

The story

Of ten virgins waiting for the bridegroom, five bring extra oil and five don't. When he arrives at midnight, the ones who ran out have gone to buy more, and the door is shut.

Why Jesus told it

This is the first of three parables in Matthew 25, spoken on the Mount of Olives in the last days before Jesus’ Passion. His disciples have asked about the end of the age and the signs of His coming.

The key

The bridegroom
Christ, who will come again
The ten virgins

the faith we profess

What it means

At a Jewish wedding, the bridegroom came at night to take his bride to his home, and the bridesmaids lit the way with torches. Delays were common; no one knew exactly when he would arrive.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism teaches that since we know neither the day nor the hour, we should follow the Lord’s advice and watch constantly, living this present time as a time of waiting and watching.

The man is not a thief. His harvest was a blessing, and planning is not a sin. What makes him a fool is that he talks only to himself (“my barns, my goods, my soul”) and never once thinks of God or of his neighbor.

The Catechism, speaking of poverty of heart, says the Lord grieves over the rich because they find their consolation in the abundance of goods.

Why Jesus told it

The story

Why Jesus told it

These are the closing words of the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), after the Beatitudes, the Our Father and Jesus’ teaching on love of enemies. The crowds, Matthew says, were astonished at His teaching.

The key

The wise builder
the one who hears Christ’s words and does them
The foolish builder
the one who hears and does nothing
The rock

What it means

In Galilee, dry riverbeds (wadis) can look like level building ground in summer. When winter rains come, they turn into rushing torrents in hours. A house built there with no deep foundation simply collapses.

Both builders heard exactly the same sermon, and from outside, both houses look fine in good weather. The difference shows only when the storm comes, and it always comes. Faith that is lived day by day, in small acts of obedience, is the foundation that holds. Faith that stays at the level of feelings and opinions does not.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism teaches that the Law of the Gospel calls for a decisive choice between the two ways and for putting the words of the Lord into practice.

What we do with the gifts, time and money God trusts us with, and why His generosity doesn’t follow our idea of fair.

The Talents

Use the gifts God gave you; fear that buries them is the real failure.Matthew 25:14–30

The story

A master going on a journey entrusts five, two and one talents to three servants. The first two double the money; the third, afraid, buries it. The master rewards the first two and rebukes the third.

Why Jesus told it

The second of the three parables in Matthew 25, told shortly before the Passion. It sits between the Ten Virgins (be ready) and the Sheep and the Goats (the judgment), and answers the question: what do we do while we wait?

The key

The master
Christ, who goes away and will return
The talents
every gift God entrusts to us: abilities, faith, time, opportunities
“To each according to his ability”
God gives each person what fits them
Trading and doubling
putting gifts to work for God and others
Burying the talent
letting fear or laziness waste what we received
“Enter into the joy of your master”
eternal life, sharing God’s own joy

What it means

A talent was not an ability but a huge sum of money, about twenty years’ wages for a laborer. Even the servant who received “only” one was trusted with a fortune. Our English word “talent” actually comes from this parable.

The first two servants receive the same praise, word for word, though one made more than the other. God does not compare our results; He looks at faithfulness. The third servant’s problem is his picture of the master: “I knew you to be a hard man... so I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground.” Fear of failure, or a false image of God as harsh, can paralyze us. The only way to lose a gift is to refuse to use it.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism teaches that our differences in talents belong to God’s plan: those endowed with particular talents are to share their benefits with those who need them.

For your life

  • What gift have I buried out of fear or comparison?
  • How do I imagine God: as a “hard man”, or as a generous Master?

As with the Talents, the Catechism sees our gifts as given by God to be shared and put at the service of others.

Told to the disciples straight after the Prodigal Son, which ends with a son who squandered property. Luke notes that the Pharisees, “who were covetous”, heard all this and mocked Him.

The key

The rich man
the owner of all things, God

What it means

This is often called the hardest of all the parables. The master praises a man who is being fired for waste and then cheats him further. Jesus is not praising the dishonesty. He is praising the steward’s clear-eyed realism: knowing his time was short, he acted decisively to secure his future.

Jesus’ point is a challenge: worldly people put enormous energy into their earthly future. Do we put as much into our eternal one? Money and possessions are not ours; we are stewards for a while. What the Church teaches

The Catechism teaches the universal destination of goods: what we own is entrusted to us to bear fruit for ourselves and for others, especially those in need.

The key

A denarius was the normal day’s wage, just enough to feed a family. Workers not hired by evening would go home with nothing. The landowner keeps going back to the marketplace because he cannot bear to see anyone left idle and empty-handed.

God the Father

sinners, the poor and the Gentiles

The Catechism teaches that everyone is called to enter the Kingdom, that it belongs above all to the poor and lowly, and that Jesus invites sinners to His table while calling them to conversion.

The Sheep and the Goats

Christ is hidden in the least of His brothers and sisters; what we do to them, we do to Him.Matthew 25:31–46

The story

When the Son of Man comes in glory, He will separate the nations as a shepherd separates sheep from goats. To those on His right He gives the Kingdom, because they fed the hungry, welcomed the stranger and visited the sick and imprisoned: "Whatever you did for one of the very least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me." Those who didn't are judged by the same words.

Why Jesus told it

The last of Jesus’ teachings before the Passion in Matthew. Right after it, He says, “after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of man will be delivered up to be crucified.”

The key

The Son of Man on His throne
Christ, the judge of all at the end of time
The sheep and the goats
the righteous and the unrighteous
The hungry, thirsty, stranger, naked, sick and imprisoned
the poor and suffering, in whom Christ is present
“You did it to me”
Christ’s identification with every person in need
The Kingdom prepared from the foundation of the world

What it means

Palestinian shepherds often grazed sheep and goats together in the day and separated them at night, because goats need more warmth. To the eye of a stranger, they could look alike.

What is striking is that both groups are surprised. The righteous did not know they were serving Christ; they were simply loving whoever was in front of them. The others did not refuse Christ knowingly; they just did not notice the needy. We are judged on love, practical and concrete. St Teresa of Calcutta summed up the Gospel on five fingers: “You did it to me.”

What the Church teaches

The Catechism teaches that we cannot be united with God if we fail to meet the serious needs of the poor and the little ones who are His brethren, and that the works of mercy are the concrete form of love.

CCC 1033, 2447

  • Which work of mercy could I take up concretely this month?
