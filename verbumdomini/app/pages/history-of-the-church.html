33-100

The Age of the Apostles

The apostles set out from Jerusalem. Peter and Paul are martyred in Rome. The writings that survive from these years show how the first Christians lived.

  1. About 33

    Pentecost

    Jerusalem, fifty days after Easter.

    The Holy Spirit came down on the apostles gathered in Jerusalem. Peter preached that day, and three thousand people were baptized. The Church counts this day as its birthday.

    Read more

  2. 67 and after

    Rome’s first leaders

    Peter’s first successors.

    After Peter and Paul were martyred in Rome, the Church of Rome was led in turn by Linus, Anacletus and Clement. Linus is named in one of Paul’s letters (2 Tim 4:21). Irenaeus wrote the list down around 180.

  3. About 96

    Clement writes to Corinth

    One church writes to correct another.

    Clement, bishop of Rome, intervened in a dispute in the Church of Corinth and asked that the priests who had been removed be restored. The apostle John was most likely still alive at the time.

  4. Late 1st century

    The Didache

    The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles.

    This short handbook describes the life of the first Christians. It plainly forbids abortion and infanticide, allows baptism by pouring water on the head three times when there is no running water, and asks for fasting on Wednesdays and Fridays.

100-451

The Church Fathers

The Church grows despite persecution. Bishops defend the faith. The councils of Nicaea and Ephesus proclaim the Creed and that Mary is the Mother of God. The list of the books of the Bible is settled.

  1. About 107

    “The Catholic Church”

    Letters from a prisoner.

    Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, wrote letters on his way to martyrdom in Rome. In them he became the first person known to use the phrase “Catholic Church”, and he described the order of bishops, priests and deacons. Tradition holds that he was a disciple of the apostle John.

  2. About 107

    Those who reject the Eucharist

    Ignatius’ letter to Smyrna.

    Ignatius wrote to the Church of Smyrna that the heretics stayed away from the Eucharist and from prayer, because they did not confess that the Eucharist is the flesh of our Savior Jesus Christ.

  3. About 112

    A Roman governor’s report

    The first Christians through pagan eyes.

    In a letter to the Emperor Trajan, the pagan governor Pliny the Younger reported that Christians met before dawn on a fixed day, sang hymns to Christ as to a god, and bound themselves by an oath (sacramentum).

  4. About 155

    The oldest description of Sunday Mass

    Justin Martyr’s First Apology.

    In his defense of the faith addressed to the emperor, the philosopher Justin described the Sunday Mass: the writings of the apostles and prophets were read, a homily was given, prayers were said, and bread and wine were offered. This food is not ordinary bread, he said; it is the flesh and blood of the incarnate Jesus.

  5. About 155

    Polycarp in Rome

    Different calendars, the same communion.

    Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna and a disciple of the apostle John, went to Rome to discuss the date of Easter with Pope Anicetus. They did not agree, but their communion was not broken: Anicetus let Polycarp celebrate the Eucharist in his own church.

  6. About 156

    Bones more precious than gold

    The earliest record of honoring relics.

    The account of Polycarp’s martyrdom says that the Christians of Smyrna gathered his bones as “more precious than precious stones and finer than gold,” and kept the day of his martyrdom every year.

  7. About 160

    Over Peter’s tomb

    The tropaion on the Vatican hill.

    On the Vatican hill in Rome, a small monument (tropaion) was built right over Peter’s tomb. The altar of Constantine’s basilica and today’s high altar stand exactly above this spot. The custom of building altars over martyrs’ tombs and placing relics in the altar stone goes back to this tradition.

  8. 2nd and 3rd centuries

    The witness of the catacombs

    Prayers to the martyrs, the first image of Mary.

    Inscriptions in the Roman catacombs ask the martyrs to pray for the living, and pray for loved ones who have died. In a fresco in the Catacomb of Priscilla, Mary appears with the Child Jesus in her lap, long before Christianity became the state religion.

  9. About 180

    Irenaeus and Rome

    Against the Heresies, Book III.

    In a work written while he was bishop of Lyon, Irenaeus of Smyrna wrote that every church must agree with the Church of Rome, founded by Peter and Paul, because of its pre-eminent authority, and he listed the bishops of Rome one by one from Peter to his own day.

  10. About 204

    The sign of the cross

    Tertullian, On the Military Crown.

    Tertullian wrote that Christians traced the sign of the cross on their foreheads whenever they began anything: setting out, bathing, sitting down to eat.

  11. About 215

    A prayer still said at Mass

    Hippolytus of Rome.

    The Apostolic Tradition, written in Rome and attributed to Hippolytus, gives the Eucharistic prayer of consecration word for word. Eucharistic Prayer II, said at Mass today, is largely based on this text.

    Read more

  12. About 244

    Infant baptism

    Origen of Alexandria.

    Origen wrote that the Church had received the tradition of baptizing infants directly from the apostles.

  13. 3rd century

    The oldest prayer to Mary

    The Sub tuum praesidium papyrus.

    A papyrus fragment found in Egypt preserves the oldest known prayer to Mary: “We fly to your protection, O Mother of God.” The prayer is still said today.

    Read more

  14. 251

    The Church as mother

    Cyprian of Carthage.

    In his work on the unity of the Church, Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, wrote that no one can have God as father who does not have the Church as mother.

  15. 325

    The Council of Nicaea

    The first form of the Creed.

    The bishops gathered at Nicaea proclaimed, against Arius, that the Son is “of one substance” (homoousios) with the Father. This Greek word, which is not found in the Bible, shows that the Church can speak in its own words to protect the faith.

  16. About 325

    Eusebius’ History of the Church

    Eusebius of Caesarea.

    In the first history of the Church, Eusebius listed the bishops of Rome, Jerusalem, Antioch and Alexandria without a break from the apostles to his own day. He also recorded how the books of the New Testament were discussed and discerned.

  17. 4th century

    The oldest complete Bibles

    Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus.

    These are the oldest great copies of the Bible that survive. Their Old Testament includes Tobit, Judith, Wisdom and the other books that Protestants later removed, alongside the rest.

    Read more

  18. About 350

    “Ask for the Catholic Church”

    Cyril of Jerusalem’s Catechetical Lectures.

    Cyril of Jerusalem advised those preparing for baptism that in a strange city they should not just ask “Where is the house of the Lord?”, because the heretics called their places that too, but “Where is the Catholic Church?”

  19. 393 and 397

    The 27 books of the New Testament

    The councils of Hippo and Carthage.

    These councils approved the 27 books of today’s New Testament and the Old Testament list, including the deuterocanonical books. The same list was proclaimed again at the councils of Florence (1442) and Trent (1546).

  20. 397

    Augustine’s words

    Against the Letter of Manichaeus.

    Augustine of Hippo wrote: I would not believe the Gospel either, if the authority of the Catholic Church did not move me to it.

    Read more

  21. About 400

    Written and unwritten tradition

    John Chrysostom on 2 Thessalonians.

    John Chrysostom said that the apostles did not hand everything down by letter, that they passed on much without writing, and that both are equally worthy of belief. “It is a tradition; seek no further.”

  22. About 427

    Perseverance to the end

    Augustine’s late writings on grace.

    Augustine wrote that even someone baptized and born again can lose salvation through grave sin, and that persevering to the end is a further grace that must be asked for.

  23. 431

    The Council of Ephesus

    Against Nestorius.

    The council gathered at Ephesus proclaimed that Mary is the “Mother of God” (Theotokos), because the child she bore is Jesus, one single person who is both God and man.

451-1500

The Middle Ages

Rome falls, and the monasteries preserve learning. East and West split in 1054. Thomas Aquinas shows that faith and reason do not conflict.

  1. 451

    The Council of Chalcedon

    Christ: one person, two natures.

    At Chalcedon, today’s Kadıköy in Istanbul, more than 500 bishops heard Pope Leo’s letter on the two natures of Christ in one person, and cried out: “Peter has spoken through Leo!”

  2. About 529

    Benedict and the monasteries

    “Pray and work.”

    Benedict of Nursia founded his monastery at Monte Cassino and wrote his Rule, built on prayer and work. After the fall of Rome, monasteries living by this Rule kept books and learning alive in the West for centuries.

    Read more

  3. 590

    Gregory the Great

    Pope from 590 to 604.

    As pope, Gregory held Rome together through plague and war, had the chants of the liturgy put in order, and in 597 sent missionaries to the English. He called himself “the servant of the servants of God,” a title the popes still use.

  4. 787

    The Second Council of Nicaea

    Against the iconoclasts.

    Meeting again at Nicaea, the council defended the veneration of icons: the honor goes not to the image but to the person it shows. Worship belongs to God alone.

  5. 1054

    East and West divide

    The Great Schism.

    Tensions between Rome and Constantinople ended in a break, with each side excommunicating the other. The excommunications were lifted in 1965 by Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras, and prayer for unity continues today.

  6. 1215

    The Fourth Lateran Council

    Called by Pope Innocent III.

    The council used the word “transubstantiation” to describe how the substance of the bread and wine is changed into the body and blood of Christ, and asked every Catholic to confess at least once a year and receive Communion at Easter.

    Read more

  7. 1265-1274

    The Summa Theologiae

    Written in Rome, Paris and Naples.

    Thomas Aquinas wrote his great work showing that faith and reason do not conflict. Six hundred years later, Pope Leo XIII made his thought the foundation of Catholic education.

    Read more

  8. 1442

    The Council of Florence

    The decree of union with the Copts.

    The council listed the books of the Bible one by one: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and Maccabees were on the list. So the 73-book Bible was the Church’s official list a century before the Reformation.

  9. About 1455

    The Gutenberg Bible

    Mainz, Germany.

    The first great book printed on a press was the Latin Vulgate Bible, the Bible of the Catholic Church. The deuterocanonical books are in it too.

1517-1600

The Reformation

Luther’s 95 Theses divide Western Christianity. The Church renews itself at the Council of Trent.

  1. 1517

    The 95 Theses

    The Reformation begins.

    The Augustinian priest Martin Luther published his 95 theses on indulgences in Wittenberg. What started as a call to debate ended up dividing Western Christianity.

  2. 1522

    Luther’s New Testament

    Letters moved to the end, “alone” added.

    In his German New Testament, Luther called the Letter of James “an epistle of straw” and put Hebrews, James, Jude and Revelation unnumbered at the end of the book. In Romans 3:28 he also added the word “alone” (allein), which is not in the Greek: “justified by faith alone.”

  3. 1529

    Luther and Zwingli

    The Marburg Colloquy.

    At Marburg, Zwingli argued that the Lord’s Supper is only a symbol. Luther insisted that Christ is truly present in the bread, and chalked on the table: “Hoc est corpus meum,” this is my body.

  4. 1520s and 1530s

    The Reformers and Mary

    What Luther and Zwingli kept.

    All his life Luther believed that Mary remained a virgin before, during and after giving birth. Zwingli too held that Mary was ever-virgin and called her the “Mother of God.”

  5. 1534

    The Luther Bible

    The deuterocanonical books between the Testaments.

    Luther’s complete German Bible did not throw out the deuterocanonical books; he placed them between the two Testaments under the heading “books not held equal to Holy Scripture, but useful and good to read.”

  6. 1536

    The death of Tyndale

    The first printed English New Testament.

    William Tyndale, who translated the Bible into English, was executed for heresy by imperial authorities at Vilvoorde, in today’s Belgium. Two years earlier England had broken with Rome over Henry VIII’s divorce; Henry too had banned Tyndale’s translation.

  7. 1541-1564

    Calvin’s Geneva

    A city run by strict rules.

    Calvin wrote that the “brothers” of Jesus were his relatives and that Mary remained a virgin. In Calvin’s Geneva, missing services, dancing or opposing his teaching was punished with fines, prison or exile; Servetus was burned for heresy in 1553.

  8. 1545-1563

    The Council of Trent

    The answer to the Reformation.

    The Church undertook a great renewal from within: the 73-book Bible, the seven sacraments and the unity of faith and works were proclaimed again, and seminaries were founded to train priests.

  9. 1571

    Lepanto

    A victory won with the Rosary.

    At the naval battle of Lepanto, Pope Pius V called on all Christians to pray the Rosary. After the victory he declared 7 October the feast of Our Lady of the Rosary.

    Read more

1800-1965

The Modern Age

Two dogmas about Mary are proclaimed, the rights of workers are defended, and totalitarian regimes are condemned. The Second Vatican Council calls everyone to holiness.

  1. 1826

    The books taken out of the Bible

    The 66-book Protestant Bible.

    After a long debate, the largest Bible society in the Protestant world decided to stop supporting editions that included the deuterocanonical books. From then on, Protestant Bibles were printed with 66 books instead of 73.

  2. 1845

    Newman becomes Catholic

    Oxford, 1845.

    John Henry Newman, one of Anglicanism’s most brilliant defenders, became a Catholic while studying the early Church, and wrote: “To be deep in history is to cease to be a Protestant.”

  3. 1854

    The Immaculate Conception

    Ineffabilis Deus.

    Pope Pius IX proclaimed as a dogma that Mary was preserved from every stain of original sin from the first moment of her conception. Four years later at Lourdes, Mary introduced herself to Bernadette: “I am the Immaculate Conception.”

    Read more

  4. 1870

    The First Vatican Council

    Pastor Aeternus.

    The council defined papal infallibility: the pope is protected from error only when, from the chair of Peter, he proclaims a teaching on faith or morals that binds the whole Church. His personal opinions and everyday decisions are not covered.

  5. 1879

    Aeterni Patris

    Leo XIII’s encyclical on philosophy.

    Pope Leo XIII made the philosophy of Thomas Aquinas the foundation of seminary training and of the defense of the faith once again.

    Read more

  6. 1891

    Rerum Novarum

    On capital and labor.

    This encyclical of Leo XIII began Catholic social teaching: it defended workers’ right to a just wage and to organize, and rejected both unbridled capitalism and state socialism.

  7. 1893

    Providentissimus Deus

    On the study of Holy Scripture.

    Leo XIII called Catholic Bible scholars to engage with the historical and linguistic studies of the age, while restating that Scripture is inspired and without error.

  8. 1896

    Apostolicae Curae

    After a papal commission’s study.

    Leo XIII declared Anglican orders “absolutely null and utterly void” because of the changes made to the form and intention of the rite of ordination during the English Reformation.

  9. 1910

    The Oath against Modernism

    Required of clergy until 1967.

    Pope Pius X asked priests, pastors and teachers of theology to swear an oath of loyalty to the faith against Modernism, which treated doctrine as something that changes with the times.

  10. 1917

    The Code of Canon Law

    Promulgated by Benedict XV.

    Replacing centuries of scattered rulings, the Church’s first single book of law (Codex Iuris Canonici) was published.

  11. 1929

    The Lateran Treaty

    Pius XI and the Italian government.

    The treaty signed between the Holy See and Italy established Vatican City as an independent state and settled the “Roman Question” that had begun with the fall of the Papal States in 1870.

  12. 1931

    Vatican Radio

    Founded with Guglielmo Marconi.

    Vatican Radio carried the voice of Pope Pius XI to the whole world with no government in between.

  13. 1937

    Mit brennender Sorge

    “With burning concern.”

    Pius XI had the encyclical, written in German rather than Latin, smuggled into Germany and read in every Catholic church on the same Sunday. It openly condemned Nazi racism and the totalitarian state.

  14. 1939-1950

    Peter’s tomb is found

    Ten years of excavation.

    Excavations under St Peter’s Basilica uncovered, right beneath the high altar, the 2nd-century monument and the inscriptions written in his honor. In 1950 Pope Pius XII announced that Peter’s tomb had been found.

  15. 1943

    Divino Afflante Spiritu

    Pius XII on biblical studies.

    Pope Pius XII encouraged Catholic scholars to study the Bible in its original languages, using the methods of archaeology, linguistics and history.

  16. 1950

    The Assumption of Mary

    Munificentissimus Deus.

    Pius XII proclaimed as a dogma that at the end of her earthly life Mary was taken up body and soul into heavenly glory.

    Read more

  17. 1962-1965

    The Second Vatican Council

    The twenty-first ecumenical council.

    Bishops from all over the world allowed local languages in the liturgy, strengthened dialogue with other Christians and stressed that everyone is called to holiness.

The story goes on in the lives of the saints.
