How to Become Catholic

Caravaggio · The Calling of Saint Matthew

To become Catholic, you contact a parish, meet the priest and prepare for a few months. How you prepare depends on one question: have you been baptized?

Contact a parish before September. Most parishes start their OCIA group in September or October. If you come after it has started, you may have to wait a full year for the next one. Call or email the parish office in spring or summer, so the priest has time to meet you first. Some parishes prepare people one to one instead of in a group, so if the parish website says nothing about OCIA, ask anyway.

OCIA · from autumn to Easter, sometimes longer

If you have never been baptized

The preparation of adults is called OCIA, the Order of Christian Initiation of Adults (it used to be called RCIA). In many parishes a group starts in September or October and is baptized at the Easter Vigil, about seven months later. The Church sets no fixed length, though. If you need more time, you take more time.

  1. 1

    Inquiry

    You come to meetings, ask questions and get to know the faith. You promise nothing.

    What you do: bring every question you have, including the hard ones. Start going to Sunday Mass.

  2. 2

    Rite of Acceptance into the Order of Catechumens

    Your first public step. At a Sunday Mass you say out loud that you want to become a Christian. The priest traces the sign of the cross on your forehead. From now on you are a catechumen, someone preparing for baptism.

    What you do: answer a few short questions in front of the parish, with your sponsor beside you.

  3. 3

    The Catechumenate

    The longest stage. You learn what the Church believes, how Catholics pray and how they live. You are also learning to pray yourself.

    What you do: go to the weekly sessions and to Sunday Mass. Some parishes send catechumens out after the homily to reflect on the readings.

  4. 4

    The Rite of Election

    On the first Sunday of Lent, usually at the cathedral, the bishop chooses you for baptism. Your name is written in the Book of the Elect.

    What you do: write your name in the book. Your sponsor tells the bishop you are ready.

  5. 5

    The Scrutinies

    On the 3rd, 4th and 5th Sundays of Lent the whole parish prays over you for healing and for freedom from sin. In these weeks you also receive the Creed and the Lord’s Prayer.

    What you do: stand at the front while the parish prays for you. Learn the Creed and the Lord’s Prayer by heart.

  6. 6

    The Easter Vigil

    The night before Easter Sunday, you receive three sacraments in one night. This is the night you become Catholic.

    • The Mass begins in the dark. A fire is lit outside and the Easter candle is carried into the church.
    • Baptism. Water is poured over your head three times, or you go down into the water, in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Every sin of your life is washed away.
    • You put on a white garment and receive a candle lit from the Easter candle.
    • Confirmation. The priest anoints your forehead with chrism, a holy oil, and you receive the Holy Spirit.
    • First Communion. For the first time, you receive the Body and Blood of Christ.

    What you do: bring your family and friends. Many people also take a saint as a patron for this night; ask your parish about the local custom for names.

  7. 7

    Mystagogy

    The fifty days from Easter to Pentecost. You look back at what happened to you and find your place in the parish.

    What you do: keep coming to Sunday Mass. Join something in the parish: a choir, a Bible study, a group that serves the poor.

Baptized Christians

If you were baptized in another church

If you were baptized with water in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, you are already a Christian. You are not baptized again. You are received into full communion with the Catholic Church.

  1. 1

    Talk to a priest

    Tell him which church baptized you and when. He will ask for proof of your baptism, so ask that church for a certificate.

  2. 2

    A preparation that fits you

    If you already pray, read the Bible and go to church, you may only need to learn what is different in Catholic belief and practice. If you have had little teaching, you will need longer. The priest decides with you.

  3. 3

    Confession

    Before you are received, you make your first confession, for the serious sins you remember since your baptism. The step-by-step confession guide shows how it goes.

  4. 4

    Reception

    Often at an ordinary Sunday Mass, not only at Easter. In front of the parish you say: “I believe and profess all that the holy Catholic Church believes, teaches, and proclaims to be revealed by God.” Then you are confirmed and receive your first Communion.

Questions and special cases

Good to know

Do I have to believe everything already?

No. Nobody starts with every answer. The preparation exists for your questions and doubts, so bring them. What the Church asks is that by the day you are received, you can honestly say you believe what she teaches.

Can I go to Mass before I am received?

Yes, and you should. Anyone may come to Mass. Until you are received, you do not go up for Communion. In many parishes you can go up with your arms crossed over your chest and the priest will give you a blessing. If you are not sure what happens when, the guide to the Mass walks through it.

Do I need a sponsor or godparent?

Yes. A sponsor walks with you through the preparation and stands beside you at the rites. For baptism this person is your godparent. They must be a Catholic who is at least 16, confirmed, has received Communion and lives the faith, and they cannot be your parent or spouse (canon 874). If you do not know anyone, tell the priest. The parish will find someone.

Does it cost anything?

The sacraments are free. Some parishes ask a small amount for books, and nobody is turned away for lack of money.

I am divorced and remarried, or married outside the Church

Tell the priest at your first meeting. Your marriage may need to be sorted out before you can be received. If you or your spouse were married before, that may mean asking a Church tribunal whether the first marriage was valid. This can take months, which is one more reason to start early. It does not stop you from beginning the preparation now.

What if my family is against it?

This is hard, and you are not the first. Tell the priest, so he knows what you are carrying. Be patient with your family and kind to them: they will learn more from how you live than from arguments. If you are still a minor, the parish will talk with your parents.

What if I am not sure my baptism was valid?

Every baptism is presumed valid unless shown otherwise. If there is serious doubt whether it was done with water and “in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit,” the priest gives a conditional baptism: “If you are not already baptized, I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.”

Adults who were baptized Catholic but never received any teaching, confirmation or first Communion have a preparation of their own.

Is the wait wasted time?

No. Becoming Catholic changes your whole life, and that takes time. You are not outside the Church while you wait. If you are baptized, your baptism already binds you to the Church (CCC 1271). If you are not, your wish to be baptized already joins you to her, and the Church treats you as her own (CCC 1249).

Do I need to go to confession?

If you are already baptized, yes: you go to confession before you are received. If you have never been baptized, no: baptism washes away every earlier sin. See the step-by-step confession guide.

This week

Your next steps

  1. 1
    Find your parish. Search for “Catholic church” and your town, or ask any Catholic you know. Call the office or send an email and say you want to become Catholic.
  2. 2
    Go to a Sunday Mass. Sit anywhere and follow along. Take the printable Order of the Mass with you so you know the words.
  3. 3
    Read one Gospel. Start with Mark. It is the shortest, and you can read it in two evenings.
