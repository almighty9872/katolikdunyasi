The Douay-Rheims Bible

The classic Catholic Bible in English, translated from Saint Jerome’s Latin Vulgate and revised by Bishop Challoner, with the spelling brought up to date. Modern book names and Psalm numbers; Challoner’s notes under the verses.

Books are grouped by the part of the story they tell. The numbers are chapters.

Why the Douay-Rheims reads differently

It has the same 73 books, but it was translated from Saint Jerome’s Latin Vulgate, while modern Catholic Bibles translate from the Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek. You will notice a few things:

  • Psalm numbers. Most are one lower in the Latin: Psalm 23, “The Lord is my shepherd”, is Douay Psalm 22. This site uses the modern numbers and shows the Douay number beside them.
  • Wording. In places the Latin reads differently, as in Genesis 3:15: “she shall crush your head”.
  • A few extra verses. It keeps some verses modern Bibles move to footnotes, like 1 John 5:7.
  • Tobit and Judith follow an Aramaic version, so they read noticeably differently.
  • Familiar wording. A few of the best-known verses use the wording most readers know, such as “Let there be light” and “The Lord is my shepherd”. The meaning stays that of the Latin. The verses of the Our Father and the Hail Mary keep “thy” and “thee”, as they are prayed.

Appendix: apocryphal books

Apocryphal comes from the Greek apokryphos, “hidden”. The Church uses the word for old religious writings that some once read alongside Scripture but that she has never received as the inspired Word of God. They are not part of the Bible.

The Council of Trent (1546) set out the 73 books of the Bible. The Latin Vulgate still printed three more after them, “lest they perish altogether”, and the first Douay Bible of 1609 kept them in an appendix: the Prayer of Manasseh, the king’s prayer of repentance in captivity; 3 Esdras, a retelling of the return from Babylon (also called 1 Esdras); and 4 Esdras, visions given to Ezra (also called 2 Esdras). They are shown here for study, in a public-domain English translation, modernized.

Further reading

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church keeps the largest Bible of any Christian church: 81 books in its narrower canon, and 88 or more when the church-order books of its broader New Testament are counted (the lists count them in slightly different ways). Most of its books are the same as ours. A few are found only in the Ethiopian Bible. They are not part of the Catholic Bible, but they help explain the world the New Testament was written in: Saint Jude quotes 1 Enoch (Jude 14-15).
