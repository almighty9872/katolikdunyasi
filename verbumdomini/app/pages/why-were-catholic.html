Why We're Catholic

We’re Catholic because we believe it’s true. The case has three steps: there is a God, He showed Himself in Jesus, and Jesus founded the Catholic Church. Each step can be read on its own.

Part 1 of 3

Is There a God?

The universe had a beginning, and its laws are tuned for life. Each of us also has a conscience that knows right from wrong. A Creator explains these facts better than chance does.

Objective Truth

Isn't truth different for everyone?

Opinions vary from person to person. Reality does not.

You can argue all day about whether a stove is hot, but touching it will still burn your hand. Even claiming “there is no truth” cancels itself out, because you are asking people to accept that statement as true.

Whether God exists is not a matter of personal taste. Either He is real, or He is not.

Common objection

But different societies have held different moral codes.

Most of those differences come from disagreeing about facts. Nearly every culture praises courage and justice and despises betrayal. And when we call the end of slavery “progress”, we are measuring it against a real standard of good.

The Beginning of the Universe

Couldn't the universe just exist on its own?

Science says the universe had a beginning. Everything that begins has a cause.

The first person to propose that the universe began at a single moment was a Belgian Catholic priest and physicist, Georges Lemaître (1931). Today we call it the Big Bang. If time, space, and matter began at that moment, their cause must exist outside of time and matter. “Nothing” produces nothing. Even the quantum vacuum is something, with laws of its own. A first cause outside time and matter, with the power to make a universe, is what people have always meant by God.

Common objection

Then who created God?

The argument is that everything that begins to exist has a cause. The universe began; God never began, because He is outside time. An endless chain of causes explains nothing. At some point you reach a being that needs nothing else to exist.

Fine-Tuning

Couldn't life be just a lucky accident?

If the strong nuclear force were a few percent different, stars could not burn and carbon could not form. If the cosmological constant were a little larger, no galaxy could have formed. Even the atheist astronomer Fred Hoyle wrote: “A common sense interpretation of the facts suggests that a superintellect has monkeyed with physics.”

Common objection

Maybe there are countless universes and we’re in a lucky one.

No one has ever observed another universe; the multiverse can’t be tested. Believing in infinitely many invisible universes takes more faith than believing in one Creator. And whatever churns out universes would need fine-tuning too, so the problem just moves back a step.

The Problem of Evil

If a good God exists, why is there so much suffering?

This is the hardest question. Christianity does not explain every case of suffering, but it points to Christ, who suffered and died Himself.

God gave us real freedom, because love can’t be forced. Most evil is that freedom misused. The Christian God doesn’t watch suffering from afar: in Christ He went through betrayal, torture and death, and defeated death by rising.

Common objection

An all-powerful God would remove suffering at once.

Only by taking away free will, and with it love and forgiveness. The Christian hope is that suffering will end: “He will wipe every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more” (Revelation 21:4).

The Heart's Longing

Do I really need God?

No success, no amount of money, no relationship ever fully satisfies us, which is a clue that we were made for something more.

C. S. Lewis: “If I find in myself a desire which no experience in this world can satisfy, the most probable explanation is that I was made for another world.” Augustine: “You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you.”

Common objection

That’s just a need for comfort; that’s why people invent religion.

Being thirsty doesn’t prove water is imaginary. And Christianity is a strange choice if all you want is comfort: it tells you to love your enemies and give your life away for others.

Who Is Jesus?

If there is a Creator, He may have spoken. Christianity rests on an event that can be checked like any other piece of history: the resurrection of Jesus.

The Historical Jesus

Did Jesus really live, or is He a legend?

That Jesus lived and was crucified under Pilate is accepted by almost every historian.

The Roman historian Tacitus (around AD 116) writes that Christians took their name from Christus, executed by Pontius Pilate; the Jewish historian Josephus also mentions “Jesus, who was called the Christ”. Even the agnostic New Testament scholar Bart Ehrman treats Jesus’ existence as beyond serious dispute. More than 5,800 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament survive; no other ancient work is so well preserved.

Common objection

The Gospels were written long after Jesus, by believers.

They were written within a few decades, while eyewitnesses were still alive. The earliest full lives of Alexander the Great come three hundred years after his death. And the Gospels keep details no one would invent: Peter’s denial, and women, whose testimony counted for little then, as the first witnesses of the empty tomb.

Who Jesus Claimed to Be

Couldn't Jesus just have been a good teacher?

What Jesus said about Himself rules that option out.

He forgave sins, and those present were outraged because they knew only God has that authority (Mark 2:5-7). He said “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30) and called Himself by the name God revealed to Moses, “I am” (John 8:58). A man who talks like that is a lunatic, a liar, or exactly who he says he is. His teaching and His life don’t fit the first two.

Common objection

Maybe later generations put these words in His mouth.

Less than thirty years after His death, Paul is already writing about Jesus as the Lord worshipped as God (Philippians 2:6-11). A legend would need generations to grow. Here a man is worshipped while the eyewitnesses are still alive, by people fiercely devoted to the one God.

The Resurrection

How can anyone believe a man rose from the dead?

Christianity rests on a testable historical claim: “If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile” (1 Corinthians 15:17).

Even most skeptical historians accept that Jesus died on the cross and was buried, that His tomb was found empty, and that His disciples sincerely believed they had seen Him risen. The creed in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 goes back to within a few years of the event and names more than five hundred witnesses. Within weeks, the same disciples who had been hiding in fear were preaching the resurrection openly, in the city where He was killed. James the skeptic and Paul the persecutor joined them.

Common objection

Maybe they hallucinated, or stole the body.

Five hundred people don’t share one hallucination, and hallucinations don’t empty tombs. If they had stolen the body, they died for what they knew was a lie. And their enemies could have ended the whole thing by producing the body, and no one ever produced it.

Why the Catholic Church?

If Jesus rose, what He said matters. And He didn’t leave a book behind: He left a Church, built on the apostles, that is still here. Four reasons it is the Catholic Church come first, then the hard questions.

One Church and Peter

Isn't believing in Jesus enough? Why a Church?

Jesus entrusted His teaching to a visible community with real authority.

He told Peter, “On this rock I will build my church,” and gave him the keys of the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 16:18-19). Without a shared authority, everyone reads the Bible their own way, and the thousands of separate denominations today are the result. Christ prayed that His disciples would “be one” (John 17:21). None of this means looking down on other Christians: by baptism they are our brothers and sisters in Christ, and we have plenty to learn from their faith (CCC 818). Today’s pope is the latest link in a chain of more than 260 that began with Peter and has held for two thousand years.

Common objection

The Church later turned Jesus’ simple message into an institution.

It was the other way round. The Church came before the New Testament: Jesus wrote no book, but He chose twelve apostles and gave them authority. Around AD 107, Ignatius of Antioch was already calling it “the Catholic Church”.

Scripture and Tradition

Why isn't the Bible alone enough?

The Church decided which books belong in the Bible. The New Testament was written and collected inside the Church.

The 27-book New Testament list was settled at the councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397). The Bible itself tells us to keep the teaching handed on by word of mouth: “Hold to the traditions which you were taught by us, either by word of mouth or by letter” (2 Thessalonians 2:15). Scripture, Tradition and the Church’s teaching office form one whole, coming from one source, Christ. Nowhere does the Bible say “Scripture alone.” Instead it speaks of teaching handed on by word of mouth and entrusted to faithful men (2 Timothy 2:2), and John says that not everything Jesus did was written down (John 21:25).

Common objection

So the Church puts itself above the Bible.

The Church serves Scripture (Dei Verbum 10). It guards and explains the Bible but can’t change a word of it, much as a museum can tell an original painting from a forgery and protect it without having painted it.

The Sacraments

Can water, bread and oil really have a spiritual effect?

God made us body and soul, so He gives His grace through signs we can see and touch.

Jesus touched people when He healed, used mud and water, and told His disciples to baptize, to offer the bread “in remembrance of me” and to forgive sins: “If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven” (John 20:22-23). The seven sacraments accompany every important moment of life, from birth to death. The first Christians didn’t treat the Eucharist as a symbol: Ignatius of Antioch criticized those who denied it was “the flesh of our Savior Jesus Christ”. Jesus said, “My flesh is food indeed, and my blood is drink indeed” (John 6:55). Many of His disciples could not accept this and left Him, yet He did not call them back to say He only meant a symbol (John 6:66). At the Last Supper He took the bread and said, “This is my body” (Mark 14:22).

Common objection

Isn’t this a kind of magic?

Magic tries to bend supernatural power to your will. In the sacraments nobody controls God; God keeps His own promise. Their power doesn’t depend on how holy the priest is, but on Christ’s word.

Mary and the Saints

Do Catholics worship Mary and the saints?

No. Worship belongs to God alone. We ask the saints to pray for us, the way you would ask a friend.

“The prayer of a righteous man has great power” (James 5:16), and death doesn’t separate people who are one in Christ. Mary is honored as Jesus’ mother and first disciple; she always points to her Son: “Do whatever he tells you” (John 2:5). The saints prove that the Christian life can actually be lived. Many of them, like Augustine, were great sinners first. Mary said, “Henceforth all generations will call me blessed” (Luke 1:48). God tells us to honor our father and mother (Exodus 20:12). Surely He wants His own Son’s mother honored.

Common objection

Why not pray to God directly?

We do. The heart of Catholic prayer is the Mass, offered to the Father through Christ. Asking a friend to pray for you doesn’t mean you trust God less, and neither does asking a friend in heaven.

Hard questions

Common objections to the Church, and our answers.

Scandals in the Church

How can the Church be holy with so many scandals?

The Church is holy because Christ is holy. Its members are sinners, and it exists to heal them.

Abuse and cover-ups are real, grave and shameful. The Church has to confess them and do justice to the victims. One of the Twelve betrayed Jesus and Peter denied Him three times; Christ knew His Church would be made of flawed people. A bad doctor doesn’t disprove medicine. The same Church also formed Teresa of Calcutta, Maximilian Kolbe and millions of holy people.

Common objection

It has survived two thousand years only because it is well organized.

No merely human institution has lasted twenty centuries with the same teaching through so many failed leaders, schisms and persecutions. Jesus promised: “The gates of hell shall not prevail against it” (Matthew 16:18).

Known by Its Fruit

Has religion really done humanity any good?

Hospitals, universities and the idea of human rights all have roots in the Church.

In the fourth century St. Basil of Caesarea founded a large complex for the sick and the poor; many historians count it among the first hospitals. The first great universities (Paris, Oxford, Bologna) grew up under the Church; Mendel, the father of genetics, was a friar, and Lemaître a priest. The idea that every person is made in God’s image and has equal dignity is one of the roots of human rights.

Common objection

But there were the Crusades and the Inquisition.

The Church has admitted those sins openly; St. John Paul II asked forgiveness for them in public in 2000. And the standard we judge them by is Christ’s own command to love even our enemies.

Grace and Good Works

Do you get to heaven by being a good person?

No. Salvation is God’s gift, and no one earns it. But real faith always shows up in what we do.

None of us can reach God’s perfect goodness on our own, so God made the first move. Christianity is the story of God setting out to find us, like the shepherd looking for his lost sheep (Luke 15:4-7). “Faith without works is dead” (James 2:26): good works don’t buy salvation; they answer a love already received. Salvation is worked out over a whole life: “Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling” (Philippians 2:12). That is why there is Confession after Baptism. Whoever falls along the way can repent and get up again.

Common objection

So a bad person can repent at the last minute and be saved? Is that fair?

Yes. Jesus told the criminal on the cross, “Today you will be with me in paradise” (Luke 23:43). Grace goes beyond fairness, because none of us deserves it. But repentance means a real change of heart, and no one is promised tomorrow.

Moral Truths

Why are the Church's rules so strict?

Its rules come from a whole picture of what a human being is.

Every human life, from the unborn child to the very old, is sacred. A person’s worth doesn’t depend on how useful they are. Your body is part of who you are, so sex finds its full meaning in a lifelong promise: marriage. Rails let a train reach where it is going, and the moral law leads a person to real happiness in the same way. The Church doesn’t change its teaching with the times. Until 1930 all the major Christian churches held contraception to be morally wrong. That year the Anglican Church allowed some exceptions, and in time most Protestant churches followed. The Catholic Church kept its teaching.

Common objection

As long as I don’t hurt anyone, what I do is nobody’s business.

The Church doesn’t force anyone; it invites. But we can harm ourselves, and what we do touches others in ways we don’t see. And no one who struggles is shut out: Confession exists so we can start again after every fall.

Heaven, Hell and Purgatory

How could a loving God send anyone to hell?

God forces no one into heaven or hell. Eternity is the answer we freely give in this life, made permanent.

Heaven is eternal union with God, the source of all goodness and love. Hell is what happens when someone freely and stubbornly chooses to stay away from God. As C. S. Lewis put it, its doors are locked from the inside. Purgatory is where those who die in God’s friendship, but not yet fully purified, are made ready for heaven. It is a final purification before heaven (2 Maccabees 12:45).

Common objection

Isn’t eternal punishment out of proportion to the sins of a finite life?

Hell is the state of a will that rejects God to the very end and stays that way. God keeps the door open until the last moment and wants no one to be lost (1 Timothy 2:4).

In Short

The main points of this page.

  1. “There is no truth” is itself a claim to truth. Truth is real, so the question of God has a real answer.
  2. The universe had a beginning. Whatever begins has a cause, and whatever caused time, space and matter can’t be made of them.
  3. Change gravity or the nuclear forces by a few percent and there are no stars and no people. Luck explains this poorly.
  4. You can only call something evil if real good exists. Anger at injustice assumes a real standard of justice, and that standard points to God.
  5. We get hungry because food exists and thirsty because water exists. We also want something no job, money or person can give.
  6. Tacitus, a Roman historian with no love for Christians, records that Jesus was executed under Pilate.
  7. Jesus forgave sins and said “I and the Father are one.” A good teacher who was only a man doesn’t talk like that.
  8. After the crucifixion the apostles hid in fear. Weeks later they were preaching in the same city, and most died for it. People don’t die for a story they made up.
  9. Jesus wrote no book. He chose twelve men, put Peter in charge, and promised His Church would last. It has, for 2,000 years.
  10. The Church decided which books go in the New Testament, in the 390s. Without the Church there is no Bible.
  11. Jesus said “This is my body.” Many followers left over it, and He let them go instead of saying it was only a symbol.
  12. Catholics don’t worship Mary or the saints. We ask them to pray for us, the way you’d ask a friend.
  13. Judas was one of the Twelve. Sinners in the Church are nothing new, and its teaching has outlasted every one of them.
  14. Heaven is a gift of God’s grace, and Confession lets you start again every time you fall.

Each step leads to the next: God, then Jesus, then His Church. That is why we’re Catholic.

Where to go from here

None of this is a mathematical proof on its own. Reason can bring you close to faith, but the last step is yours to take. If you have questions, you’re not alone: walk into any Catholic church, or write to us.
