OCIA

Marriage and relationship questions for people preparing to enter the Church, and what each situation needs before Easter

Every adult joining the Church is asked about their marriage. A pastor needs to know whether a marriage is valid, whether a former spouse stands in the way, and whether a couple living together is preparing to marry. The Church presumes that every marriage is valid until the contrary is proved (Canon 1060), so most situations are settled with a few documents and one conversation. This page lays out the general rules for each case. Your pastor and the diocesan tribunal decide your own.

Start with the stages of OCIA if you have not read them yet.

The four possible outcomes

OutcomeWhat it means
Clear to proceedNothing stands in the way. Give your pastor the documents he asks for and continue toward Easter.
Already validYour marriage is valid as it is. No convalidation is needed, and a valid marriage between two baptized Christians is a sacrament.
Convalidation or radical sanationA Catholic married without the Church’s form. The marriage is made valid before the sacraments are received.
Nullity or privilege firstA former marriage has not been resolved by the Church. This must be settled before a new wedding or convalidation.

Find your situation

Choose the line that describes you today. Each one shows the steps, an example and the sources.

Single and never married

Clear to proceed. A person with no prior marriage faces no marriage impediment to Baptism, Confirmation and the Eucharist.

  1. Tell your OCIA director that you have never been married.
  2. Give your parish a Christian baptismal certificate. If you were never baptized, or belonged to a group whose baptism the Church does not recognize, such as the Latter-day Saints or Jehovah’s Witnesses, a birth certificate is enough.
  3. Continue toward the Easter sacraments.

Example: Liam in Halifax has never married. He is baptized, confirmed and receives his first Communion at Easter with no marriage paperwork.

Sources: Canon 1058; Catechism 1601 and 1212; 1 Corinthians 7:32 to 34.

Widowed and single

Clear to proceed. Death ends the marriage bond, so no annulment is needed.

  1. Give your parish a copy of your late spouse’s death certificate.
  2. Continue through OCIA toward Easter.

Example: Aileen in Winnipeg was married before her husband died. She submits his death certificate and completes OCIA with no annulment and no marriage preparation.

Sources: Canons 1085 and 1141; Catechism 1640; Romans 7:2 to 3 and 1 Corinthians 7:39.

Divorced and single

Clear to proceed. A civil divorce, an Islamic Talaq or Khula, or a Jewish Get does not stop an unmarried person from entering OCIA or receiving the sacraments.

  1. Give your parish the marriage certificate and the divorce decree.
  2. No annulment is needed unless you plan to marry again. If you might, speak to your pastor early, because the former marriage has to be examined first (see “A former marriage” below).
  3. Continue toward Easter.

Example: Sonia in Brampton is divorced and single. She gives her parish her divorce decree and receives Confirmation and first Communion at Easter.

Sources: Canon 1085; Catechism 1649 and 1650; 1 Corinthians 7:10 to 11.

Engaged, not yet married

What you need depends on whether either of you has been married before.

Neither of you has been married

Marriage preparation needed. Complete OCIA, then prepare for a Catholic wedding.

  1. Meet your parish priest well ahead of the wedding. Dioceses often ask for six to twelve months.
  2. Complete the diocesan marriage preparation.
  3. If your fiancé or fiancée is baptized in another church, ask for permission for a mixed marriage. If your partner is unbaptized, ask for a dispensation for disparity of cult. Your priest requests both (Canons 1086 and 1124 to 1125).
  4. Receive your Easter sacraments before the wedding.

If either of you belongs to an Eastern Catholic Church, such as the Ukrainian, Maronite or Melkite Church, the Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches and the eparchy’s own norms apply. An Eastern Catholic marriage requires the blessing of a priest, and a deacon cannot give it. Meet the priest of your Eastern parish early.

One of you was married before and the former spouse is dead

Marriage preparation needed. Death dissolved the first marriage. Give the death certificate and the first marriage certificate to your parish, then begin marriage preparation in the usual way.

One of you was married before and the former spouse is living

Nullity or privilege first. Do not book a venue or set a date until the former marriage is resolved. See “A former marriage” below. If you are not living together, ask your pastor how OCIA timing works during the tribunal process.

Example: Noah in Quebec City is engaged to an unbaptized partner and they do not live together. He completes OCIA at Easter and prepares for a Catholic wedding with a dispensation for disparity of cult.

Sources: Canons 1085, 1086, 1108 and 1124 to 1125; Catechism 1631 to 1637 and 1640; Mark 10:7 to 9.

Living together, not married

A pastoral step is needed. The Church teaches that sexual union belongs inside marriage (Catechism 2350 and 2390), so this is settled before the Easter sacraments.

  1. Meet your priest or OCIA director right away and in private.
  2. Confirm that neither of you has a prior marriage that was never resolved. If either does, see “A former marriage” below.
  3. Choose one of two paths: prepare to marry in the Church, or end the shared living arrangement before the sacraments.

Example: Evan in Edmonton has lived with his partner for three years. He meets his pastor to plan a Catholic wedding.

Sources: Canons 1055 and 1066; Catechism 2350, 2353 and 2390; 1 Corinthians 6:18 to 20.

Married, and neither of you was Catholic at the wedding

Already valid. Only Catholics are bound to the Catholic form of marriage. Valid marriages of Protestants, Muslims, Jews, Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists and unbaptized people are recognized as valid, so no convalidation is needed. A valid marriage becomes a sacrament when both spouses have a valid Christian baptism.

Give your parish the marriage certificate and confirm that neither of you had an earlier marriage that was never resolved. If one of you had, see “A former marriage” below. Then find the line that fits you:

  • Both unbaptized, and only you are in OCIA. Your marriage is a valid natural marriage. You receive Baptism, Confirmation and the Eucharist at Easter, and your spouse does not have to join. If your spouse is baptized later, the marriage becomes sacramental with no second ceremony.
  • Both unbaptized, and both in OCIA. At the Easter Vigil, when you are both baptized, your marriage becomes a sacrament at that moment. You may ask your pastor for a simple blessing afterward.
  • One validly baptized Christian and one unbaptized spouse. Your marriage is a valid natural marriage. It becomes sacramental when the unbaptized spouse is validly baptized. Give your parish the baptized spouse’s certificate.
  • Both validly baptized Christians. Your marriage is valid and already a sacrament. The Church recognizes baptism with water in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. It does not recognize baptism in groups that deny the Trinity, such as the Latter-day Saints or Jehovah’s Witnesses.
  • A traditional, tribal or religious wedding abroad with no civil papers. If neither of you was Catholic and neither had a prior bond, the original ceremony is a valid natural marriage. Give your parish whatever record exists, including a later civil registration. Do not schedule a convalidation.
  • A proxy wedding, or one under pressure from family. A marriage by proxy can be valid when a proper mandate was freely given (Canon 1105). A marriage entered under grave fear or coercion is invalid (Canon 1103). If you consented freely, nothing more is needed. If you were forced, talk to your pastor about the diocesan tribunal, privately.
  • Records are lost, or the archives cannot be reached. Refugees and immigrants are not turned away. Give your pastor any immigration papers that show your marital status, together with sworn statements from two witnesses who knew you abroad. Baptism can be proved by a witness above suspicion or by the sworn word of the person baptized (Canon 876).

Example: Priya and Connor in Calgary had a civil wedding while both were unbaptized, and neither had married before. Both complete OCIA and are baptized at the Easter Vigil. Their marriage was valid already and is now a sacrament.

Sources: Canons 1055, 1059, 1061, 1103, 1105, 1108 and 876; Catechism 1625 to 1628 and 1638; Genesis 2:24; 1 Corinthians 7:12 to 14.

Married, and at least one of you was Catholic at the wedding

A baptized Catholic must marry before a priest or deacon with two witnesses, unless the bishop dispensed from that form (Canons 1108 and 1117). This holds even if the Catholic was never confirmed or had stopped practicing. How your wedding was celebrated decides the outcome.

  • Married in a Catholic church before a priest or deacon. Already valid. Give your parish the marriage certificate, or a baptismal certificate with the marriage noted, and continue toward Easter. An Eastern Catholic wedding is equally valid and follows the Eastern Churches’ own norms.
  • Married outside the Church with a bishop’s dispensation. Already valid. Give your parish the record of the dispensation.
  • Married at a courthouse, a hall, on a beach or in a non-Catholic church, with no dispensation. The marriage lacks canonical form and is not valid in the Church. See the three remedies below.

Convalidation

Required before the sacraments. When both spouses are willing, they renew their consent before a priest or deacon and two witnesses (Canons 1156 to 1160).

  1. Meet your parish priest immediately, before Lent.
  2. Gather a recent baptismal certificate for the Catholic spouse, the civil marriage record and the names of two witnesses.
  3. Complete marriage preparation and obtain any needed permission for a mixed marriage or dispensation for disparity of cult.
  4. Celebrate the convalidation before the sacraments.

A blessing is not enough

A devotional blessing of the marriage does not replace convalidation. The priest can explain the difference between a blessing and the Rite of Convalidation.

Radical sanation

If a spouse will not take part in a Catholic ceremony, the pastor can ask the bishop for a radical sanation (sanatio in radice, Canons 1161 to 1165). The bishop makes the marriage valid without a new ceremony, provided that the consent of both spouses continues. Confirm that neither of you has an unresolved prior marriage, and include any needed dispensation in the request.

Example: Zoe in Moncton was baptized Catholic and had a civil wedding with no parish involvement. During OCIA she and her husband complete preparation and celebrate a convalidation before Easter.

Sources: Canons 1108, 1117 and 1156 to 1165; Catechism 1625 to 1631; Mark 10:9; 1 Corinthians 7:12 to 16.

A former marriage that is still living, or was never resolved

Nullity or privilege first. Marriage lasts for life, and a first marriage is presumed valid. A civil divorce, a civil annulment, an Islamic Talaq or Khula and a Jewish Get end the marriage in civil or religious law, but they do not end it in the Catholic Church (Catechism 1640 and 1650). Until the Church has resolved the former marriage, a new wedding or a convalidation is blocked. A person who remains single can still complete OCIA normally.

The first step is the same for everyone: bring the marriage certificate and the divorce papers to your pastor and begin early, because the process takes time. Keep attending Mass and OCIA while it runs. Your reception, wedding or convalidation is scheduled when the decision arrives. The kind of decision depends on how the former marriage began.

  • A Catholic who married outside the Church without a dispensation. A faster documentary process often applies, called a declaration of nullity for lack of form (Canon 1688). Gather the Catholic’s recent baptismal certificate, the marriage record and the divorce decree.
  • Two unbaptized spouses, one of whom enters OCIA, and the other will not live in peace with the Christian. The Pauline Privilege (Canons 1143 to 1147). Once the convert is baptized, the former marriage is dissolved at the moment the new marriage is celebrated. The bishop usually asks the unbaptized former spouse whether they will be baptized or live peacefully, unless that is impossible or useless. You will need proof that neither of you was baptized during the marriage.
  • A marriage in which one spouse was baptized and the other never was. The Favor of the Faith, sometimes called the Petrine Privilege. The diocesan tribunal prepares the petition, and the Pope grants it through the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith. You will need baptismal records, proof that the other spouse was never baptized, the marriage certificate and the divorce decree. The petitioner must not have been the blameworthy cause of the breakup. Wait for the Pope’s rescript before the new marriage is celebrated.
  • A marriage that was never consummated. A valid marriage that was never consummated can be dissolved by the Pope for a just cause (Canon 1142). Your pastor and the judicial vicar start the petition, and you provide the marriage and divorce records and proof that the marriage was never consummated.
  • A marriage that ended only with a civil annulment. A civil court’s annulment has no effect in Church law. The tribunal must still examine the marriage.
  • Any other marriage, including marriages of non-Catholics and marriages celebrated in the Church. A formal petition for a declaration of nullity (Canons 1673 to 1680). Collect the marriage certificate, the divorce decree and the names of witnesses.

If a person is already living in a second union without an annulment, the pastor and a tribunal advocate review the first marriage before the second can be convalidated and before the sacraments. In some cases a couple may be permitted to receive the sacraments if they live together as brother and sister. The pastor will explain whether that applies.

Example: Tariq in Mississauga was married in an Islamic Nikah and divorced by Talaq. He enters OCIA hoping to marry a Catholic woman. He gives his records to the diocesan tribunal so it can examine nullity or the Pauline Privilege before a wedding date is set.

Sources: Canons 1060, 1085, 1141 to 1147, 1673 to 1680 and 1688; Catechism 1629, 1640 and 1650; Matthew 19:6 to 9; Mark 10:11 to 12; 1 Corinthians 7:12 to 15.

A plural marriage entered before baptism

Special norm. An unbaptized man who has several unbaptized wives and receives baptism may keep the one he married first. If keeping her is a hardship, he may keep one of the others, and he must separate justly from the rest (Canon 1148).

  1. Speak in confidence with your pastor and your bishop early in OCIA.
  2. After baptism, marry the chosen spouse in canonical form, observing the norms for a mixed marriage if she remains unbaptized.
  3. Meet every duty of justice, housing and financial support toward the other wives and the children.

Example: Emmanuel in Edmonton made two customary marriages in West Africa while unbaptized. He works with his pastor and bishop to remain married to one wife in canonical form and to provide justly for the other.

Sources: Canon 1148; Catechism 1645 and 2387; Genesis 2:24; Matthew 19:4 to 6.

Terms you will hear

Canonical form
The Church’s required way for a Catholic to marry: before the local bishop, the pastor, or a priest or deacon delegated by them, and two witnesses.
Convalidation
A renewal of consent in canonical form that makes an invalid marriage valid.
Radical sanation
The bishop’s act that makes an invalid marriage valid without a new ceremony.
Declaration of nullity
A tribunal’s finding that a marriage was never valid. It is often called an annulment. It does not make children illegitimate and does not deny that a real relationship existed.
Pauline Privilege and Petrine Privilege
Two ways the Church dissolves a marriage in which at least one spouse was never baptized, in favor of the faith.
Disparity of cult
A marriage between a Catholic and an unbaptized person. It needs a dispensation.
Mixed marriage
A marriage between a Catholic and a baptized non-Catholic. It needs the bishop’s permission.
