# Writing Style Guide (English site)

This guide covers text written for the English site (verbumdomini.ca, everything in `verbumdomini/`). Apply it to text you write or edit; don't rewrite existing pages for style unless asked. Capitalized pronouns for God (He, Him, His, Himself in our own text) are covered in `docs/english-site.md` under Ground rules.

Write in plain, concrete, matter-of-fact English. Every sentence should earn its place by delivering facts, evidence, history or clear instructions without rhetorical padding, synthetic cadence or repetitive templates.

---

## 1. Core Voice and Punctuation

- **Tone:** Calm, direct and objective. Write like a knowledgeable reference editor, not a marketer, motivational speaker or lecturer.
- **Impersonal perspective:** Use direct statements or attribute teachings to the proper authority ("The Church teaches...", "The council defined...") rather than self-referential editorial "we" phrasing ("Here we explain...", "Below we try to show...").
- **No apologies for clarity:** State sensitive, contested or difficult positions plainly in one or two calm sentences. Do not open sections with defensive preambles or disclaimers apologizing for stating a position clearly.
- **Punctuation limits:** Never use em dashes, exclamation points or decorative symbols. Use commas, parentheses, colons and periods.
- **Serial comma:** Don't use it: "Scripture, tradition and the magisterium", not "Scripture, tradition, and the magisterium".
- **Primary source integrity:** When editing surrounding commentary, never alter verbatim quotes, Scripture, prayers, historical texts or citations.

---

## 2. Sentence Rhythm and Syntax

### Break up symmetrical triplets and quadruplets
Avoid stacking three-part or four-part parallel lists in back-to-back sentences. Use pairs, single concrete statements or asymmetric phrasing.

- **Avoid:** "There are no ads, no accounts, no paywall and no tracking. It is for the curious, for anyone on the way into the Church, and for Catholics who want to know their faith better."
- **Prefer:** "Everything is free to read, with no ads or tracking. It is written for newcomers and lifelong Catholics alike."

### Eliminate repetitive "not X, but Y" contrasts
State positive claims directly instead of defining things by what they are not.

- **Avoid:** "The question of God is the same: not a matter of taste, but a real question with a real answer."
- **Prefer:** "The existence of God works the same way. It is a factual question with a real answer."
- **Avoid:** "This page is not an attack. It is an invitation."
- **Prefer:** "This page invites Jewish readers to examine these texts directly."

### Vary sentence length and smooth out staccato closers
Combine choppy, uniform short sentences into natural compound structures. Replace clipped two- or three-word mic-drop fragments at the end of paragraphs with complete sentences or merge them into the preceding clause.

- **Avoid:** "Nobody thinks that." / "They never did." / "Both are fine." / "Atheism can't supply one."
- **Prefer:** "Atheism has no objective basis for that standard." / "Either option is fine."

## 3. Structural Economy and Non-Repetition

### Never repeat a thesis across structural layers
Each layer of a page (kicker, heading, subtitle, preview summary and opening body sentence) must add new information. If a subtitle or card preview already states the main point, delete the duplicate restatement in the first sentence of the body and move straight into evidence, history or citations.

- **Avoid:** Subtitle: "If the universe's settings were slightly different, there would be no stars, and no us." followed immediately by Body: "The basic constants of the universe seem tuned with astonishing precision to allow life."
- **Prefer:** Keep the subtitle and start the body directly with the physical evidence: "If the strong nuclear force were a few percent different, stars could not burn and carbon could not form."

### Deduplicate kickers and headings
Do not place a small kicker above a heading when the kicker merely repeats the heading in different words ("Common questions / Common misunderstandings", "The texts / The sixteen documents", "Your calendar / When could you be received?"). Drop the redundant kicker or use a genuine structural label ("Part 1", "Documents").

### Keep previews concise and non-overlapping
When a page uses an expandable card or FAQ preview, limit the preview to one concise sentence. Start the expanded body directly with historical context, primary sources or theological reasoning without repeating the preview wording.

### Avoid triple-layer repetition on timeline and history cards
Do not state the same event or quote in the card title, the card subtitle and the opening sentence of the body. Let the title name the event, the subtitle add a distinct detail or consequence and the body narrate what happened.

## 4. Transitions, Openers and Framing

### Cut stage-direction intros and colon announcements
Let arguments flow from one paragraph into the next without teacher-like signposts.

- **Avoid:** "Let's run the test:" / "The dilemma:" / "The main replies:" / "Our answer is this:"
- **Prefer:** Move straight into the premise or objection.

### Replace echo-question transitions with declarative topic sentences
Do not open paragraphs or sub-sections with clipped fragment questions followed by "No."

- **Avoid:** "Changed? No, and you can check it yourself." / "Kept from the people? No." / "Bible alone? The Bible never says..."
- **Prefer:** "Ancient manuscripts confirm the text has not changed." / "The Church never hid Scripture from ordinary readers."

### Delete warm-up filler and truisms
Remove conversational throat-clearing and obvious aphorisms when a direct quote or argument already makes the point.

- **Avoid:** "Then there's simple logic." / "You don't need to be a philosopher to know that." / "In court, the strongest evidence is an admission from the other side." / "Hunger means there is food; thirst means there is water."

### Remove theatrical buildup around analogies
State thought experiments and philosophical examples cleanly without stage directions or exaggerated reactions.

- **Avoid:** "You're walking in a forest with a friend and find some shiny spheres... Either he's joking or he's lost it. Now let the spheres grow. Make them the size of a stadium."
- **Prefer:** State the principle of contingent existence directly and give the example in one or two plain sentences.

### Restrict bold lead-in fragments
Do not open every paragraph with a bolded two-to-four-word noun phrase followed by a period ("Christ's own plan.", "Three kinds of honor.", "One and three, but not in the same way."). Reserve bold lead-ins for distinct multi-part items inside a single answer; open standard paragraphs with normal sentences.

## 5. Attribution, Analogies and Historical Tense

### Introduce people plainly by field or role
Drop stacked superlative bios and institutional prestige tags before names.

- **Avoid:** "one of the most respected atheist philosophers alive, Graham Oppy" / "America's best-known skeptical New Testament scholar, Bart Ehrman" / "the Oxford-educated atheist Alex O'Connor"
- **Prefer:** "philosopher Graham Oppy" / "New Testament scholar Bart Ehrman" / "writer Alex O'Connor"

### Use everyday similes sparingly
Do not rely on repetitive domestic comparisons ("is like asking a friend to pray for you", "just as kissing a photo of someone you love isn't about the paper") when Scripture, history or primary sources already explain the concept.

### Use standard past tense for history
Narrate past events in standard past tense rather than dramatic present-tense documentary narration.

- **Avoid:** "The Holy Spirit comes down and the Church is born. Peter preaches that day..."
- **Prefer:** "The Holy Spirit came down on the apostles in Jerusalem. Peter preached that day, and three thousand people were baptized."

### Replace melodramatic taglines with factual descriptions
- **Avoid:** "The Creed is born." / "Everything starts with the sign of the cross."
- **Prefer:** "Nicaea adopted the first form of the Creed." / "Tertullian described Christians signing their foreheads throughout the day."

## 6. Guides, Cards and Batch-Generated Entries

### Weave actions naturally into multi-step guides
Do not append an identical micro-label (such as "What you do:") to the bottom of every numbered step. Fold simple instructions directly into the step's paragraph, and use a separate callout only when a step requires advance preparation.

### Omit synthetic reflection prompts and redundant moral boxes
Do not end informational, historical or scriptural entries with first-person journaling questions ("For your life: Could I pray one decade today? Where do I feel too small...") or separate "What it means" summary boxes that restate the story as an aphorism. Fold any essential historical takeaway into the main narrative or teaching section.

### Use symbol-decoder lists only for explicit allegories
Only include a point-by-point symbol key when the primary text itself provides an explicit allegorical breakdown. For straightforward narrative examples or brief images, go directly from the summary into the explanation.

### Vary sentence templates across batch entries
When writing multiple biographies, profiles or catalog cards, vary sentence openings and structure so entries do not repeat an identical skeleton ("[Name] is known for X, Y and Z. He/She is the patron saint of...").

### Keep UI microcopy and onboarding concrete
Replace vague descriptions with specific details about what the reader can do or read.

- **Avoid:** "One person writes and maintains it, and it keeps growing." / "The step-by-step confession guide shows how it goes."
- **Prefer:** "Written and maintained by one person, with new guides and texts added regularly." / "The confession guide walks through every prayer and response in order."
